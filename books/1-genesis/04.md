# Genesis 4

**1** And the human knew Eve his woman, and she conceived and bore Cain, and she said, "I have gotten a man with the LORD."

**2** And she bore again, his brother Abel. And Abel was a herder of flocks, and Cain was a worker of the ground.

**3** And it came to pass at the end of days that Cain brought from the fruit of the ground an offering to the LORD.

**4** And Abel, he too brought from the firstborn of his flock, from their fat portions. And the LORD looked upon Abel and upon his offering,

**5** but upon Cain and upon his offering He did not look. And it burned in Cain greatly, and his face fell.

**6** And the LORD said to Cain, "Why has it burned in you, and why has your face fallen?

**7** Is it not so — if you do well, lifting up; and if you do not do well, sin is crouching at the door. And toward you is its longing, but you — you must rule over it."

## The Field

**8** And Cain said to Abel his brother… And it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him.

**9** And the LORD said to Cain, "Where is Abel your brother?" And he said, "I do not know. Am I my brother's keeper?"

**10** And He said, "What have you done? The voice of your brother's bloods is crying out to Me from the ground.

**11** And now, cursed are you from the ground, which opened its mouth to take your brother's bloods from your hand.

**12** When you work the ground, it will not go on giving its strength to you. A wanderer and a wavering one you shall be in the earth."

**13** And Cain said to the LORD, "My guilt is greater than I can carry.

**14** Look — You have driven me out this day from upon the face of the ground, and from Your face I shall be hidden; and I shall be a wanderer and a wavering one in the earth, and it will be that whoever finds me will kill me."

**15** And the LORD said to him, "Therefore, whoever kills Cain, sevenfold it shall be avenged." And the LORD set a sign for Cain, so that whoever found him would not strike him.

**16** And Cain went out from before the face of the LORD, and dwelt in the land of Nod, east of Eden.

## The Line of Cain

**17** And Cain knew his woman, and she conceived and bore Enoch. And he was building a city, and he called the name of the city after the name of his son, Enoch.

**18** And to Enoch was born Irad, and Irad fathered Mehujael, and Mehujael fathered Methushael, and Methushael fathered Lamech.

**19** And Lamech took for himself two women: the name of the one was Adah, and the name of the second, Zillah.

**20** And Adah bore Jabal; he was the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock.

**21** And the name of his brother was Jubal; he was the father of all who handle the lyre and the pipe.

**22** And Zillah, she too bore Tubal-cain, a sharpener of every cutting tool of bronze and iron. And the sister of Tubal-cain was Naamah.

**23** And Lamech said to his women,  
"Adah and Zillah, hear my voice;  
women of Lamech, give ear to my saying.  
For a man I have killed for wounding me,  
and a boy for bruising me.

**24** For sevenfold Cain is avenged,  
and Lamech seventy-seven."

**25** And the human knew his woman again, and she bore a son and called his name Seth, "for God has set for me another seed in place of Abel, because Cain killed him."

**26** And to Seth also a son was born, and he called his name Enosh. Then it was begun, calling on the name of the LORD.

## Notes

- **v1 "knew"** — *yada*, and it is not a coy substitute for a word Hebrew lacked. Hebrew has a second verb, *shakhav*, to lie down with, and Genesis uses it freely. Which one appears is not random.

  *Yada* is used of Adam and Eve here, of Cain and his wife in v17, of Adam and Eve again in v25 — and each time a named child follows. It is the word for a virgin as one who *has not known* a man (19:8, 24:16), and for Judah, who *knew her no more* (38:26).

  *Shakhav* appears where the act sits outside the bond it belongs in: Lot's daughters after making him drunk (19:32–35), Shechem and Dinah (34:2), Reuben and his father's concubine (35:22), Leah hiring Jacob for a night with mandrakes (30:15–16), Potiphar's wife (39:7, 12, 14). Forced, stolen, deceived, or bought — and only the mandrakes make it a bargain, so the thread is not commerce.

  But the sharper point is that *shakhav* is **not a word for sex at all**. Of its nineteen appearances in Genesis, several are simply lying down: the men of Sodom *had not yet lain down* for the night (19:4), Jacob *lies down* at Bethel with a stone under his head (28:11), *the land on which you are lying* (28:13), and Jacob asking to *lie with my fathers*, meaning to die (47:30). It means to lie down. Context supplies the rest.

  Which is the finding worth keeping: **Hebrew has no dedicated word for sex.** It has *know* and *lie down*, two entirely ordinary verbs, and the writer chooses which ordinary human act to name. That is why *yada* carries what it carries — not a polite substitute for a blunt word, but the word for entering into something and being changed by it, pointed at a marriage. Rendering it "slept with" would reach for the other verb and erase a choice the author made across fifty chapters.

  It is also why *making love* is not the equivalent, though it is the nearest modern phrase. That expression is about **feeling** — warmth, mutuality, romance. *Yada* is not about feeling. "Adam knew Eve" says nothing of tenderness; it says the union was real and had consequences, that something was entered into which he now carries.

  Finally, this is a strong tendency and not a law. 19:5 breaks it, where a mob demanding two strangers says *let us **know** them* — the covenant word, for the opposite of a covenant.

  And *yada* is not a narrow word pressed into service. It is the same verb as the tree of the **knowing** of good and evil (2:17), and as *now I **know** that you fear God* (22:12). Hebrew knowing is not information; it is what you have entered into and been changed by. "Knew his wife" sits in the same family as knowing God, and that is the claim being made.
- **v1 "Cain"** — Heb. *Qayin*, and Eve's word for what she has done is *qaniti*, "I have gotten / acquired / produced." The name and the verb chime. She is not describing him; she is punning on him.
- **v1 "a man with the LORD"** — *ish et-YHWH*, three words that nobody can settle. *Et* is either the preposition "with" or the untranslatable particle Hebrew puts before a direct object. So the line reads "I have gotten a man with the LORD's help," or "I have gotten a man, namely the LORD," or "I have gotten a man along with the LORD." English Bibles pick one and print it smoothly. The Hebrew does not resolve, and the first thing a mother says about the first child born is genuinely unclear.
- **v2 "Abel"** — Heb. *Hevel*, and in Hebrew it is not a name at all. It is a common noun: **vapor, breath, mist — the thing that is gone as you say it.** It is the word that opens Ecclesiastes, *hevel havalim*, printed in English as "vanity of vanities" but closer to *breath of breaths*. The second human being ever born is called Fleeting, and he lasts eight verses.

  "Abel" is the same word, worn down by travel. The Greek translators wrote *Hevel* as Ἄβελ, losing the initial *h* their alphabet could not carry there; Latin took the Greek, and English took the Latin. So nothing was mistranslated — the sound simply eroded across two alphabets until the meaning stopped being audible. Three thousand years of readers have known him as Abel, and the name stands here. What English cannot do is let you hear his mother name him Vapor.
- **v2 "a worker of the ground"** — *'oved adamah*, the same pairing from 2:5 and 2:15, where the human was made to *serve* the ground. Cain is doing what humans were made for. That is worth holding on to when the offering is refused: the text gives no reason, and the older reader's instinct to supply one — wrong attitude, wrong produce — is the reader's, not the text's.
- **v5 "it burned in Cain"** — *va-yichar le-Qayin*, literally "and it burned to Cain." Hebrew locates anger as heat inside a person, with no stated subject. "Cain was very angry" is accurate and colder than the Hebrew.
- **v7** — one of the hardest verses in the book, and no honest rendering is smooth. "If you do well, lifting up" is three words in Hebrew, *se'et*, an infinitive with no object; lifting of the face, probably, answering the fallen face in v5, but it is not said.
- **v7 "sin is crouching at the door"** — *robets*, a participle used of an animal lying in wait. There is an Akkadian word, *rabisu*, naming a demon that lurks at doorways, and the resemblance is close enough that many readers hear a crouching thing rather than an abstraction. Note also that *sin* is feminine in Hebrew while *crouching* here is masculine — the grammar is already uneven, which is part of why the verse is so contested.
- **v7 "toward you is its longing, but you must rule over it"** — *teshuqah* and *mashal*, the same two words as 3:16, where the woman's longing is toward her man and he rules over her. Four verses apart in a short book, the identical uncommon pair. Whatever it means in one place constrains what it can mean in the other, and English translations that print "desire" in 3:16 and something else here sever the link.
- **v8 "And Cain said to Abel his brother…"** — the Hebrew has the verb *va-yomer*, "and he said," and then no speech. The sentence moves straight to the field. Three things are worth knowing, and none of them is that the text is damaged.

  First, it may be **deliberate**. The words Cain used to get his brother alone are withheld from the reader. That would not be out of character for this writer: 3:22 breaks off God's own sentence unfinished — "lest he put out his hand and take also from the tree of life, and eat, and live forever —" — at the moment of greatest weight. Twice in two chapters, language is cut away exactly where the reader most wants it. Once is an accident; twice looks like a hand. The ellipsis here is an attempt to let the English reader feel the withholding instead of having it explained.

  Second, **most published Bibles simply render it "Cain spoke with his brother"** or "talked with Abel his brother," reading *amar el* as *conversed with* rather than as introducing a quotation. On that reading nothing is missing at all: the verse reports that a conversation happened and does not undertake to quote it. This is the King James reading and it is grammatically defensible.

  Third, several ancient translations — Samaritan, Greek, Latin, Syriac — supply "let us go out to the field," and some modern Bibles print that with a footnote. Whether the Hebrew lost the line or the translations added it is contested, and the usual principle that the harder reading is the older one argues the Hebrew here is original.
- **v10 "your brother's bloods"** — *demei*, plural. Hebrew uses the plural of blood for bloodshed, blood that has been spilled and is owed. Not a poetic flourish; a legal one.
- **v12, 16 "a wanderer and a wavering one… the land of Nod"** — *na' va-nad*, a rhyming pair like *tohu wa-vohu* in 1:2, where the sound is half the meaning. Then in v16 he settles in *Nod* — built on *nad*, the second half of his own sentence. The place is named Wandering. He is sentenced to a word and then goes to live inside it, and English, which must keep the received name, can only report that the two are the same root.
- **v13 "My guilt is greater than I can carry"** — *'avoni*, which means both the iniquity and the punishment for it, and *nasa*, which means both to bear and to forgive. So the line is simultaneously "my punishment is too heavy" and "my guilt is too great to be forgiven." English must choose. Hebrew does not, and Cain's first words after the murder are a sentence that can be read as self-pity or as confession.
- **v15 "a sign for Cain"** — *'ot le-Qayin*, a sign **for** him, not necessarily **on** him. The Hebrew never says it was put on his body; "the mark of Cain" is a later reading hardened into an English idiom. A sign given *to* someone, for their protection, is the plain sense, and the verse says the sign's purpose is to stop people killing him — it is a mercy, not a brand.
- **vv17–18 the line of Cain, against the line of Seth** — these six names and the seven in chapter 5 are not two unrelated lists. Set them side by side:

  Cain — Enoch — Irad — Mehujael — Methushael — Lamech
  Kenan — Mahalalel — Jared — **Enoch** — Methuselah — **Lamech**

  **Enoch** is *Chanokh* in both, letter for letter. **Lamech** is *Lemekh* in both, letter for letter. They are two different men each time, in two different families. The rest are near-misses rather than matches: *Qayin* and *Qeynan* differ by a final letter; *Irad* is *Yered* with one letter in front; *Metusha'el* and *Metushelach* share their first four letters. Whatever is happening here — two tellings of one memory, or a deliberate pairing of the doomed line against the kept one — the two lists are built from nearly the same set of sounds.

  And the pairs that share a name are opposites. Cain's Enoch has a **city** named after him (v17); Seth's Enoch walks with God and *is not, for God took him* (5:24). Cain's Lamech boasts of killing a man and claims seventy-sevenfold vengeance (vv23–24); Seth's Lamech names his son for the relief of the curse (5:29). The same two names, in the same order, on men who could not be less alike.
- **v18 "Mehujael"** — the Hebrew spells this man's name **twice in one verse, differently**: *Mechuya'el* and then *Mechiya'el*. The scribes copied both and left them. It is a small thing and it is exactly the kind of small thing the Masoretes would not tidy.
- **v23–24 Lamech's song** — the first poetry spoken by a human being in the Bible, and it is a boast about killing a boy. Set as lines because the Hebrew is parallel verse. Where God set Cain's avenging at sevenfold, Lamech claims seventy-seven for himself: five generations from the first murder, the arithmetic of revenge has multiplied elevenfold, and nobody has commented on it.
- **v26 "Then it was begun"** — *az huchal*, a passive with no subject. Something started; the text does not say who started it.
