# Genesis 35

**1** And God said to Jacob, "Rise, go up to Bethel and settle there, and make there an altar to the God who appeared to you when you fled from the face of Esau your brother."

**2** And Jacob said to his household and to all who were with him, "Remove the foreign gods that are among you, and purify yourselves, and change your clothes.

**3** And let us rise and go up to Bethel, and let me make there an altar to the God who answered me in the day of my distress, and who was with me on the road I walked."

**4** And they gave Jacob all the foreign gods that were in their hand, and the rings that were in their ears, and Jacob hid them under the terebinth that is by Shechem.

**5** And they pulled up. And a terror of God was on the cities around them, and they did not pursue after the sons of Jacob.

**6** And Jacob came to Luz, which is in the land of Canaan — that is Bethel — he and all the people who were with him.

**7** And he built there an altar and called the place El-Bethel, because there God had been revealed to him when he fled from the face of his brother.

**8** And Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died, and she was buried below Bethel, under the oak. And he called its name Allon-bacuth.

## Your Name Is Jacob

**9** And God appeared to Jacob again, on his coming from Paddan-aram, and blessed him.

**10** And God said to him, "Your name is Jacob. Your name shall no longer be called Jacob — Israel shall be your name." And He called his name Israel.

**11** And God said to him, "I am God Almighty. Be fruitful and multiply. A nation and an assembly of nations shall come from you, and kings shall come out of your loins.

**12** And the land that I gave to Abraham and to Isaac, to you I will give it, and to your seed after you I will give the land."

**13** And God went up from upon him, in the place where He had spoken with him.

**14** And Jacob set up a pillar in the place where He had spoken with him, a pillar of stone, and he poured out a drink offering on it and poured oil on it.

**15** And Jacob called the name of the place where God had spoken with him Bethel.

## On the Road to Ephrath

**16** And they pulled up from Bethel. And there was still a stretch of ground to come to Ephrath, and Rachel gave birth, and her labour was hard.

**17** And it came to pass, when her labour was at its hardest, that the midwife said to her, "Do not be afraid, for this one also is a son for you."

**18** And it came to pass, as her soul was going out — for she was dying — that she called his name Ben-oni. And his father called him Benjamin.

**19** And Rachel died, and she was buried on the road to Ephrath — that is Bethlehem.

**20** And Jacob set up a pillar over her grave. That is the pillar of Rachel's grave, to this day.

**21** And Israel pulled up and pitched his tent beyond Migdal-eder.

**22** And it came to pass, while Israel was dwelling in that land, that Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his father's secondary wife. And Israel heard. And the sons of Jacob were twelve.

**23** The sons of Leah: Reuben, Jacob's firstborn, and Simeon and Levi and Judah and Issachar and Zebulun.

**24** The sons of Rachel: Joseph and Benjamin.

**25** And the sons of Bilhah, Rachel's servant girl: Dan and Naphtali.

**26** And the sons of Zilpah, Leah's servant girl: Gad and Asher. These are the sons of Jacob who were born to him in Paddan-aram.

**27** And Jacob came to Isaac his father at Mamre, Kiriath-arba — that is Hebron — where Abraham and Isaac had lived as foreigners.

**28** And the days of Isaac were a hundred years and eighty years.

**29** And Isaac breathed his last and died, and was gathered to his people, old and full of days. And Esau and Jacob his sons buried him.

## Notes

- **v1 "the God who appeared to you when you fled from the face of Esau"** — God identifies himself to Jacob by the worst night of Jacob's life. Not *the God of Abraham*, not *the God who made you rich in Paddan-aram*: the God of the night you ran with nothing. And v7 says it a second time, *when he fled from the face of his brother*. The chapter will not let the twenty prosperous years stand in for that stone pillow.
- **v2 "the foreign gods that are among you"** — *elohei ha-nekhar*, and Jacob's household has them. Rachel took her father's household gods in 31:19 and lied to his face about them; here they are being handed over, or some of them are, along with *ha-nezamim asher be-ozneihem*, the rings in their ears, which suggests amulets rather than jewelry. The text does not say whether Rachel's are in the pile.
- **v4 "Jacob hid them"** — *va-yitmon*, to bury out of sight, to conceal. He does not smash them or burn them, which is what Israel is told to do with such things everywhere else. He hides them **under the terebinth that is by Shechem** — under a named tree, at the city his sons had just emptied, in ground they do not own. The verb and the location are both exact, and neither one is destruction.
- **v3 "in the day of my distress"** — *be-yom tsarati*, from *tsarar*, to press in, to be tight. It is the noun of the verb in 32:7, *va-yetser lo*, **and it was narrow for him**, on the night before he met Esau. Jacob names God by the same pressure twice in three verses.
- **v5 "a terror of God"** — *chittat Elohim*, from *chatat*, to be shattered or broken with fear. The noun is rare. After chapter 34 the surrounding cities have every reason to come after Jacob's sons, and the one sentence the narrator gives for why they did not is this. Nobody does anything; something falls on the cities.
- **v7 "God had been revealed to him"** — *niglu elav ha-Elohim*, and the verb *niglu* is **plural**. *Elohim* is a plural-shaped word that takes singular verbs almost without exception in the Hebrew Bible — *bara Elohim*, God created, singular, in 1:1. In a small handful of places, and this is one, the verb agrees with the plural form instead. What it means is not decidable from the grammar: it may be an old idiom, it may be attraction to the plural ending. What can be said is that it is unusual and that the scribes left it standing.
- **v8 "Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died"** — this woman has not been mentioned since 24:59, where Rebekah's unnamed nurse went with her to Canaan; she is named here for the first and only time, in the verse where she dies. And Rebekah herself never gets such a verse. The Bible records the deaths of Sarah, of Abraham, of Rachel, of Isaac, of Jacob — and never narrates Rebekah's. The only funeral in her family the text stops for is her nurse's, under a tree called **Allon-bacuth**, *the oak of weeping*.
- **v10 "Your name is Jacob. Your name shall no longer be called Jacob"** — the sentence starts by confirming the old name and then cancels it, and the renaming of 32:28 is done over again, this time by God in daylight rather than by a man who would not give his name in the dark.

  And it does not take. Compare Abram, who after 17:5 is never called Abram again in the entire Bible. Jacob is called Jacob in v20 of this chapter, ten verses after this, and the narrator swaps between the two names freely for the rest of the book — *Israel* pulled up in v21, *the sons of **Jacob*** in v22. He is the only person in the Bible who keeps both names, and he keeps the one that means heel-grabber.
- **v11 "God Almighty"** — *El Shaddai*, the name that came with the covenant of circumcision in 17:1 (see the note there for what the word does and does not support). What is new here is what follows: *goy u-qehal goyim*, **a nation and an assembly of nations**. *Qahal* is the word for the gathered community of Israel, and it is the word the Greek Old Testament renders *ekklēsia*, which is where English gets **church**. Its first appearance in the Bible is this promise, said to a man on a road with eleven sons and a dying wife.
- **v14 "a pillar of stone"** — he does exactly what he did at this spot in 28:18, on the way out: set up a stone, pour oil on it. The addition is *va-yassekh aleha nesekh*, he poured a **drink offering** on it — the first libation in the Bible. Twenty years and two chapters of God-encounters later, the ritual has grown by one element, at the same rock.
- **v16 "a stretch of ground"** — *kivrat ha-arets*, a measure of distance that occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible and is never defined. Nothing in the language fixes it. It means some distance short of arriving, and every English translation that prints a figure is supplying one.
- **v17 "this one also is a son for you"** — the midwife's line answers a sentence from five chapters back. In 30:24 Rachel named Joseph *may the LORD **add** me another son*. The midwife tells her, while she is dying, that the prayer was granted, and the word she uses, *gam*, **also**, is the hinge: this one too. Rachel got what she asked for in 30:1 — *give me sons, and if not, I am dead* — and the text has now given her both halves of that sentence.
- **v18 "as her soul was going out"** — *be-tset nafshah*. *Nefesh* is not a soul in the later sense; it is breath, throat, appetite, life itself — what leaves when a body stops. The Hebrew is describing the last exhalation, and it is one of very few places in the Torah where a death is watched from inside.
- **v18 "Ben-oni... Benjamin"** — *Ben-Oni* is genuinely two words. *On* can be **sorrow**, and it can be **strength, vigour, generative power** — in 49:3 Jacob calls Reuben *my strength and the first of my **on***, plainly the second sense. So the dying woman's name for her son is either *son of my suffering* or *son of my strength*, and Hebrew does not choose. Then Jacob overrides her: *Binyamin*, **son of the right hand** — the favoured side, the strong side, and also the south, since Hebrew orients by facing east.

  This is the only child in the Bible whose mother names him and whose father immediately renames him. And the man doing the renaming is the one who wrestled all night for a name of his own and was told *your name shall no longer be called Jacob*.
- **v20 "to this day"** — *ad ha-yom*, and it is the narrator speaking to his own readers, pointing at a marker they could go and look at. Jacob has now set up three pillars: at Bethel going out (28:18), on the heap with Laban (31:45), and at Bethel again in v14 of this chapter. The fourth is a gravestone.
- **v22 "And Israel heard."** — and then the sentence stops. There is no reaction, no rebuke, no scene; what comes next in the same verse is a headcount, *and the sons of Jacob were twelve*, and after that a list of their names. Jacob hears that his firstborn has slept with his wife's servant — the woman who bore Dan and Naphtali — and the narrator gives him three words and moves on. It is the second time in two chapters that Jacob is told something terrible and says nothing: in 34:5 *Jacob kept silent until they came*.

  He answers both eventually, in the same speech, on his deathbed. Reuben in 49:4: *unstable as water, you shall not excel, for you went up to your father's bed*. Simeon and Levi in 49:5–7. Everything Jacob does not say in chapters 34 and 35 comes out fourteen chapters later, all at once, as curses.
- **v22 "And the sons of Jacob were twelve"** — placed immediately after the sentence that ruins the firstborn, and immediately before a list that still names Reuben first. The count is announced at the exact moment the order inside it has stopped meaning what it says.
- **v26 "born to him in Paddan-aram"** — Benjamin was born eight verses earlier, on the road to Ephrath, in Canaan. The summary says all twelve were born in Paddan-aram and it is not true of the one the chapter just finished narrating. The text leaves the two statements standing next to each other without adjusting either, and this rendering does the same.
- **v29 "And Esau and Jacob his sons buried him"** — Esau is named first. When Abraham was buried in 25:9 it was *Isaac and Ishmael his sons* — the son of the promise first, the elder second. Here the order flips: the elder goes first, and the brother who bought the birthright and stole the blessing is named second at his father's grave. Isaac lives to a hundred and eighty, longer than Abraham, which means he was alive through every chapter of this story — through the twenty years in Paddan-aram, through Shechem, through Rachel's death — and the text has not mentioned him since chapter 28. He was blind and thought he was dying then. He outlasts the entire quarrel.
