# Genesis 12

**1** The LORD said to Abram, "Go, for yourself, from your land, and from your kindred, and from your father's house, to the land that I will show you.

**2** I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you, and I will make your name great. Be a blessing.

**3** I will bless those who bless you, and the one who treats you lightly I will curse. In you all the families of the ground shall be blessed."

**4** Abram went, as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. Abram was seventy-five years old when he went out from Harran.

**5** Abram took Sarai his wife, and Lot his brother's son, and all their possessions that they had gathered, and the souls they had made in Harran, and they went out to go to the land of Canaan. They came to the land of Canaan.

**6** Abram passed through the land as far as the place of Shechem, as far as the oak of Moreh. The Canaanite was then in the land.

**7** The LORD appeared to Abram and said, "To your seed I will give this land." He built there an altar to the LORD who had appeared to him.

**8** He moved on from there to the hill country east of Bethel, and he pitched his tent — Bethel on the west and Ai on the east. He built there an altar to the LORD, and he called on the name of the LORD.

**9** Abram journeyed, going and journeying, toward the Negev.

## Down to Egypt

**10** There was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to live there as a guest, for the famine was heavy in the land.

**11** And it came to pass, as he drew near to come into Egypt, that he said to Sarai his wife, "Look now — I know that you are a woman beautiful to look at.

**12** It will be, when the Egyptians see you, that they will say, 'This is his wife,' and they will kill me, and you they will keep alive.

**13** Say, please, that you are my sister, so that it may go well with me on your account, and my soul may live because of you."

**14** And it came to pass, when Abram came into Egypt, that the Egyptians saw the woman, that she was very beautiful.

**15** The officials of Pharaoh saw her, and they praised her to Pharaoh, and the woman was taken into Pharaoh's house.

**16** He dealt well with Abram on her account, and he had flocks and cattle and donkeys and male servants and female servants and she-donkeys and camels.

**17** The LORD struck Pharaoh with great strikes, and his house also, over the matter of Sarai, Abram's wife.

**18** Pharaoh called Abram and said, "What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife?

**19** Why did you say, 'She is my sister,' so that I took her for myself as a wife? And now — here is your wife. Take her and go."

**20** Pharaoh gave men orders concerning him, and they sent him away, and his wife, and all that was his.

## Notes

- **v1 "Go, for yourself"** — *lekh lekha*, two words, and the second one has defeated every translator. It is the verb *go* followed by a preposition meaning *to you* or *for you* — a construction Hebrew uses to throw the action back on the one doing it. "Go by yourself," "go for your own sake," "get yourself gone," "go, you." Most English Bibles print "Go" and drop the second word entirely. Something is being said to Abram about himself in the command, and it cannot be carried over.
- **v1 "from your land, and from your kindred, and from your father's house"** — three phrases, and they narrow. Country, then clan, then the household you sleep in. If they ran the other way it would be a list; running this way it is a tightening, each step costing more than the one before, ending on the smallest and hardest thing to leave.
- **v1 "the land that I will show you"** — *asher ar'ekka*. He is not told where. The destination is withheld in the same sentence that commands the departure, and the verb is future: *that I will show you*, not *that I have shown you*.
- **vv2–3 "bless"** — the root *barakh* five times in two verses, after the previous eleven chapters have handed out curse after curse — the serpent, the ground, Cain, Canaan. The word turns over here and stays turned for the rest of the book.
- **v2 "a great nation"** — *goy gadol*, and English "great" sends a modern reader the wrong way. It does not mean powerful, and it does not mean a people who rule over others. *Gadol* here means **large**. The Hebrew says so itself in 18:18, where the same promise is expanded to *goy gadol **ve-atsum***, a great and **numerous** nation — the second word supplies the sense of the first.

  And *goy* is not a country with a government. It is a population that has become numerous and distinct enough to count as a people at all. Which is the whole point of saying it to this man: Abram is seventy-five and childless. He is not being offered subjects. He is being told that he, who has no children, will become a people.

  46:3 settles it past argument. God tells Jacob not to fear going down to Egypt, *for I will make you a great nation **there*** — in Egypt, where his descendants will be slaves for four hundred years. Exodus 1:7 then reports it done, in the same vocabulary: the sons of Israel *were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied and grew **numerous** beyond measure* — *va-ya'atsmu*, the word from 18:18. The promise of a great nation is fulfilled on a slave population, before a single one of them rules anything.
- **v2 "I will make your name great"** — *va-agaddelah shemekha*. Eight verses earlier, the builders at Babel said *let us make for ourselves a name* — *na'aseh lanu shem*. Same noun, opposite direction. They seized; he is given. The two stories are placed back to back so that the second answers the first, and an English reader who cannot see *shem* in both sentences will never notice the reply.
- **v2 "Be a blessing"** — *ve-heyeh berakhah*, an imperative. Not "you will be blessed" but a command to be one. It sits at the end of a list of things God will do, and hands one back.
- **v3 "the one who treats you lightly"** — *u-meqallelkha*, from *qalal*, whose root sense is to be light, slight, of no weight. Hebrew has a heavier word for cursing, *arar*, and it uses that one in the very next clause for what God will do. So the sentence is not symmetrical: those who make you *light* I will *curse*. English prints "curse" twice and loses the imbalance.
- **v3 "in you all the families of the ground shall be blessed"** — *ve-nivrekhu vekha*. The verb form can be passive (*shall be blessed*) or reflexive (*shall bless themselves by you* — that is, shall use your name when they pronounce a blessing). Both are grammatically available and the difference is large. Also note it is *families of the ground*, *adamah*, not "of the earth" — the ground that was cursed in 3:17 because of the man.
- **v5 "the souls they had made in Harran"** — *ha-nephesh asher asu be-Charan*. An odd phrase. *Nephesh* is the throat-word rendered "living soul" throughout chapters 1–2; *asu* is simply "made." People they acquired, or people they somehow made. The Hebrew does not explain itself, and translations that print "the people they had acquired" are choosing.
- **v6 "The Canaanite was then in the land"** — the narrator stepping outside the story to tell the reader something about the time it happened. The word is *az*, then — which implies a *now* when it is no longer so.
- **vv10–20** — read the sequence and then read the book of Exodus: a famine drives the family down to Egypt; the Egyptian ruler takes what is not his; the LORD strikes Pharaoh with *nega'im*, great strikes or plagues; and Pharaoh **sends them away** — *va-yeshallechu*, the verb Moses will use again and again — with livestock, silver and servants they did not have when they came. The whole Exodus is in these eleven verses, in miniature, four hundred years early.
- **v13 "so that it may go well with me on your account, and my soul may live because of you"** — two clauses, both about him, both ending in *you*. He is asking her to carry the risk and states the benefit twice, in his own name.
- **vv14–16** — Sarai does not speak. She is seen, praised, taken, and Abram is paid, and she says nothing in any of it. Neither does he.
