# Genesis 3

**1** Now the serpent was shrewder than every living thing of the field that the LORD God had made. And it said to the woman, "So God has said, 'You shall not eat from any tree of the garden'?"

**2** And the woman said to the serpent, "From the fruit of the trees of the garden we may eat.

**3** But from the fruit of the tree that is in the middle of the garden, God has said, 'You shall not eat from it, and you shall not touch it, or you will die.'"

**4** And the serpent said to the woman, "Dying, you shall not die.

**5** For God knows that on the day you eat from it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like gods, knowing good and evil."

**6** And the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and the tree was desirable for gaining insight; and she took from its fruit and ate, and she gave also to her man with her, and he ate.

**7** And the eyes of the two of them were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves coverings.

## The Voice in the Garden

**8** And they heard the sound of the LORD God walking about in the garden at the breeze of the day, and the human and his woman hid themselves from the face of the LORD God among the trees of the garden.

**9** And the LORD God called to the human and said to him, "Where are you?"

**10** And he said, "I heard Your sound in the garden, and I was afraid, because I am naked, and I hid myself."

**11** And He said, "Who told you that you are naked? Have you eaten from the tree that I charged you not to eat from?"

**12** And the human said, "The woman whom You gave to be with me — she gave me from the tree, and I ate."

**13** And the LORD God said to the woman, "What is this you have done?" And the woman said, "The serpent deceived me, and I ate."

## The Sentences

**14** And the LORD God said to the serpent, "Because you have done this, cursed are you above all beasts and above every living thing of the field. Upon your belly you shall go, and dust you shall eat all the days of your life.

**15** And I will put hostility between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. He shall strike at your head, and you shall strike at his heel."

**16** To the woman He said, "I will greatly increase your pain and your conceiving; in pain you shall bear children. And toward your man shall be your longing, and he shall rule over you."

**17** And to the human He said, "Because you listened to the voice of your woman and ate from the tree that I charged you, saying, 'You shall not eat from it' — cursed is the ground on your account. In pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life.

**18** And thorn and thistle it shall sprout for you, and you shall eat the plants of the field.

**19** By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, until you return to the ground, for from it you were taken. For dust you are, and to dust you shall return."

**20** And the human called his woman's name Eve, because she was the mother of all living.

**21** And the LORD God made for the human and for his woman tunics of skin, and clothed them.

**22** And the LORD God said, "Look — the human has become like one of us, knowing good and evil. And now — in case he puts out his hand and take also from the tree of life, and eat, and live forever —"

**23** So the LORD God sent him away from the garden of Eden, to serve the ground from which he had been taken.

**24** And He drove the human out, and He made dwell east of the garden of Eden the cherubim, and the flame of the turning sword, to keep the way to the tree of life.

## Notes

- **v1 "shrewder"** — Heb. *'arum*. The chapter before ended with the man and woman *'arummim*, naked. The two words sound nearly identical and are unrelated in meaning. Hebrew narrative works by this kind of hinge, and it is doing real work: the story turns on nakedness and on cunning, and the writer has bolted them together with a pun on the seam between two chapters. No English translation can carry it; the note is the only place it survives.
- **v1 "shrewd"** — *'arum* is not a wicked word. Proverbs uses it approvingly, of the prudent man who sees trouble coming. "Crafty" and "subtle" import a verdict the Hebrew has not yet passed.
- **v1 "the serpent"** — *nachash*. An ordinary snake; it is called one of the animals "the LORD God had made," in this same verse. Nothing in this chapter identifies it with anything else. Later traditions do; the text does not, and the note's job is to say where the text stops.
- **v1 "So God has said…?"** — the Hebrew opening *aph ki* is broken and hard to construe, almost a half-sentence — something like "so, then, God said…?" It is not the smooth question English Bibles supply. The serpent begins mid-insinuation, and it begins by overstating the rule so the woman will correct it.
- **v3 "and you shall not touch it"** — this was not in the command. Compare 2:17. Someone has added a fence around the law, and the text lets it stand without comment.
- **v4 "Dying, you shall not die"** — *lo mot temutun*, the serpent taking God's exact construction from 2:17 (*mot tamut*) and putting a negative in front of it. It is not making a new claim; it is quoting the sentence back with one word changed.
- **v5 "like gods"** — *k-elohim*, with a plural participle following. *Elohim* is grammatically plural and means both "God" and "gods," and the grammar here leans plural. In v22 God says the human "has become like **one of us**," which points the same direction. Most English Bibles print "like God"; the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous and the ambiguity is worth preserving rather than resolving.
- **v6 "desirable for gaining insight"** — *nechmad le-haskil*. *Haskil* is to gain skill, to succeed through understanding. The appeal is not appetite but competence — the wish to be good at existing.
- **v8 "the sound of the LORD God walking about"** — *qol YHWH Elohim mithhallekh*. *Qol* is sound or voice; *mithhallekh* is the form used of strolling, moving about habitually. And "the breeze of the day" is *le-ruach hayom* — the same *ruach* that hovered over the water in 1:2, here just the evening wind. The scene is domestic: someone taking a familiar walk at the cool part of the day.
- **v9 "Where are you?"** — *ayyekkah*. One word in Hebrew.
- **v15 "He shall strike at your head, and you shall strike at his heel"** — the same verb, *shuph*, is used both times, and nobody is certain what it means. Proposals run from "crush" to "snap at" to "lie in wait for." English Bibles often print two different verbs — *bruise* the head, *strike* the heel — which manufactures a distinction the Hebrew does not make. One word, twice, meaning uncertain: that is the honest report.
- **v16 "your pain and your conceiving"** — *'itsavon* is the word here, and it is the **same word** used of the man in v17 for his labor in the field. English usually prints "pain" for her and "toil" for him, which splits one Hebrew word into two English ones and hides that the two sentences are built from the same material.
- **v16 "your longing"** — *teshuqah*, a rare word, occurring three times in the Bible. One of the other three is 4:7, where sin's *teshuqah* is toward Cain and he must rule over it. Readers should know the nearest parallel and be left to weigh it.
- **v17 "cursed is the ground on your account"** — the ground, *adamah*, is cursed; the man, *adam*, is not. Neither is the woman. In this chapter only the serpent and the soil are cursed directly.
- **v19 "for dust you are, and to dust you shall return"** — closing the loop from 2:7, where he was shaped of dust from the ground. The sentence is not an added punishment so much as the undoing of an assembly.
- **v20 "Eve"** — Heb. *Chavvah*, built on *chay*, alive. The verse explains the name — he calls her this **because** she is the mother of all *chay*, all living — so in Hebrew the sentence closes on itself. In English it cannot: the name and the reason are unrelated sounds.

  Worth hearing what became of it. *Chavvah* is two syllables with a *v* in the middle, opening on a *chet*, a scrape at the back of the throat that Greek had no letter for. Greek dropped the throat-sound, Latin wrote **Eva**, and most of Europe kept that — Eva in Spanish, Italian, German, Polish. English is the outlier: it took a shortened Old French form, so the last vowel fell away and the *v* slid to the end. **Eva is nearer the Hebrew than Eve**, keeping both syllables and the *v* where the Hebrew puts it. A name wore down further in one language than in the others.
- **v22** — the sentence breaks off unfinished: "lest he put out his hand and take also from the tree of life, and eat, and live forever —". Hebrew leaves it hanging. Most English Bibles close it with a dash or supply an ending. The break is in the original.
- **v24 "to keep the way"** — *lishmor*, the same verb given to the man in 2:15, where he was put in the garden *to serve it and to keep it*. He was the garden's guard. Now the garden is guarded from him, and the job he was given is being done by something else. One word closes the circle, and English uses "keep" in both places without anything marking that it is the same assignment changing hands.
- **v24 "the cherubim"** — *ha-keruvim*, and note the definite article: "**the** cherubim," at this word's first appearance in the Bible, as though the reader already knows what they are.

  Almost everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible the word names a **made object**. Cherubim are hammered out of gold on the cover of the ark, carved from olive wood and overlaid for the temple, woven into curtains, engraved on walls and doors. Exodus, Kings, and Chronicles together account for over eighty such occurrences — every one of them a manufactured thing. In the surrounding cultures, winged composite guardian figures were statues set flanking a doorway to guard it, which is exactly what this verse describes. Ezekiel's visions, by contrast, show cherubim that move and fly. Genesis does not say which these are.

  What the grammar does settle is how many things are stationed. The object marker *et* appears twice — *et ha-keruvim* and *et lahat ha-cherev* — so the cherubim and the flame of the turning sword are the two direct objects of "He stationed," and they are what does the guarding. No third party stands in the sentence, and no hand holds the sword.

  Read them as made figures and the verse does not empty out; it locks into place. Exodus 25 has Israel hammer two cherubim of gold from the ends of the ark's cover, wings spread over it, faces toward each other — and then God says, *I will meet with you there, and I will speak with you from above the cover, from between the two cherubim*. Cherubim of beaten gold are not an absence. They mark the exact spot where God says He will be found. Exodus 26 then has cherubim worked into the veil, the curtain across the one doorway in Israel's worship that must not be walked through.

  Which is this verse. A threshold that may not be crossed, marked by cherubim, with the way to life on the other side. Eden's gate and the Holy of Holies are the same arrangement, and everything Israel later builds to approach God is built in the shape of the door they were put out of.

  English "cherubim" carries none of this, and misleads further still: the word now summons the winged infants of Renaissance painting, an image with no basis in any of these texts.
- **v24 "the flame of the turning sword"** — *lahat ha-cherev ha-mithhappeket*, a sword-flame that turns itself every way. Grammatically it stands on its own, not held by anyone.
