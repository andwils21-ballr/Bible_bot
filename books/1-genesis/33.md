# Genesis 33

**1** And Jacob lifted his eyes and looked, and here — Esau coming, and four hundred men with him. And he divided the children between Leah and Rachel and the two servant girls.

**2** And he put the servant girls and their children first, and Leah and her children after them, and Rachel and Joseph last.

**3** And he himself crossed over ahead of them and bowed to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother.

**4** And Esau ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept.

**5** And he lifted his eyes and saw the women and the children, and said, "Who are these to you?" And he said, "The children with whom God has favored your servant."

**6** And the servant girls came near, they and their children, and bowed down.

**7** And Leah also came near, and her children, and they bowed down. And afterward Joseph came near, and Rachel, and they bowed down.

**8** And he said, "What to you is all this camp that I met?" And he said, "To find favor in the eyes of my lord."

**9** And Esau said, "I have much, my brother. Let what is yours be yours."

**10** And Jacob said, "No, please — if I have found favor in your eyes, then take my gift from my hand. For this is why I have seen your face, as one sees the face of God, and you have received me.

**11** Take, please, my blessing that has been brought to you — for God has favored me, and because I have everything." And he pressed him, and he took it.

## Not to Seir

**12** And he said, "Let us pull up and go, and I will go alongside you."

**13** And he said to him, "My lord knows that the children are tender, and the flocks and the herds are nursing upon me. If they are driven hard one day, all the flock will die.

**14** Let my lord cross over ahead of his servant, and I will lead on at my own slowness, at the pace of the property that is before me and at the pace of the children, until I come to my lord at Seir."

**15** And Esau said, "Let me set with you some of the people who are with me." And he said, "Why this? Let me find favor in the eyes of my lord."

**16** And Esau returned that day on his way to Seir.

**17** And Jacob pulled up to Succoth and built himself a house, and for his livestock he made booths. Therefore he called the name of the place Succoth.

**18** And Jacob came whole to the city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan, on his coming from Paddan-aram. And he camped before the face of the city.

**19** And he bought the portion of the field where he had pitched his tent from the hand of the sons of Hamor, father of Shechem, for a hundred qesitah.

**20** And he set up an altar there and called it El, God of Israel.

## Notes

- **v2 the order of the line** — servant girls and their children first, Leah and hers second, Rachel and Joseph last. He is arranging his family in the order he can afford to lose them, and the text says so without saying so: it gives the order, and nothing else. This is the same man who split his herds into two camps in the previous chapter so that *the camp that is left will be an escape*. Bilhah and Zilpah's four sons are the front rank.
- **v3 "bowed to the ground seven times"** — *va-yishtachu artsah sheva pe'amim*, the full prostration, repeated, which is the protocol for approaching a king. And the verb is the one from his father's blessing: in 27:29 Isaac told Jacob *be lord over your brothers, and let your mother's sons **bow down** to you* — *ve-yishtachavu lekha* — the same verb, *shachah*. He got that blessing by pretending to be Esau. The first thing he does on meeting him is perform its exact reverse, seven times.

  He keeps it up all chapter. He calls Esau *adoni*, **my lord**, five times, and himself *avdekha*, **your servant**. The blessing said the brothers would serve him. He spends twenty verses saying the opposite out loud.
- **v4 "and kissed him"** — this word carries a mark you can see on the page. In the Hebrew manuscripts *va-yishaqehu* has a **dot written over every one of its letters**. There are a handful of such places in the Hebrew Bible, and they are one of the oldest surviving scribal signals in the text — the scribes copied the word and flagged it, and then copied the flags for two thousand years.

  What the flag means is not written down anywhere in the text. Later readers have argued both directions, that the kiss was wholehearted and that it was not, and the dots do not settle it. What can be said with certainty is that at this word, and not at the embrace or the falling on the neck or the weeping that surround it, somebody very early had something to mark, and every copy since has kept the marking without explaining it.
- **v5 "Who are these to you?"** — *mi elleh lakh*. Jacob has now been asked who he is in three consecutive scenes: *who are you, my son* by his blind father in 27:18, *what is your name* by the man at the ford in 32:27, and now this. The first time he lied. The second time he told the truth and was renamed. This time the question is about his children, and he answers by naming God: *the children with whom God has **favored** your servant* — *chanan*, to show unearned favor, the root behind the name *Hannah* and behind *Yochanan*, which becomes John.
- **vv9, 11 "much" against "everything"** — Esau says *yesh li rav*, **I have much**. Jacob says *yesh li khol*, **I have everything**. One word apart, and they are the two brothers in miniature: the one who has enough and the one who cannot say enough. Jacob is the one who left with a stick.
- **v10 "as one sees the face of God"** — *kir'ot pnei Elohim*, and the sentence is deliberate. He named a place *Peniel*, face of God, the night before, because *I have seen God face to face and my life was delivered* (32:30). Now he looks at his brother and uses the same words. *Va-tirtseni*, **and you have received me** — *ratsah* is the verb used of God accepting a sacrifice as acceptable. He is telling Esau that surviving him and surviving God were the same experience.
- **v11 "my blessing"** — *birkhati*, and this is the chapter's hinge. Every earlier verse called the drove a *minchah*, a **gift** or tribute (32:13, 32:18, 33:10). Here Jacob changes the word. *Berakhah* is the thing he took from Esau in chapter 27 by dressing in goatskins — and what he is handing over is two hundred goats, two hundred ewes, camels, cattle and donkeys. He gives the blessing back, in the form of livestock, using its own name. And the verb is *hubat lakh*, **brought to you**, a passive with no agent, as though nobody were doing it.

  Then *va-yiftsar bo va-yiqqach* — he pressed him, and he took it. Three words. Esau does not want it and accepts it anyway, and the transaction that has driven the whole story since chapter 25 closes in half a verse.
- **v13 "nursing upon me"** — *alot alai*. The flocks are giving milk and the young are on them, so the herd moves at the speed of the slowest animal. It is a true reason and a convenient one.
- **v14 "at my own slowness"** — *le-itti*, from a root meaning gentleness or slowness, a rare word. And twice in one sentence *le-regel*, **at the foot of** — at the pace of the property, at the pace of the children. Jacob is explaining, carefully and at length, why he cannot travel with his brother.
- **vv14, 16, 17 Seir and Succoth** — he says *until I come to my lord at **Seir***. Esau leaves for Seir. Jacob goes to **Succoth**, in the other direction, and builds a house. The narrator sets the three sentences side by side and adds nothing. There is no rebuke, no explanation, and no later scene in which he arrives at Seir. It is simply what he said and what he did, reported in the same flat voice as the herds and the tents.
- **v17 "Succoth"** — *sukkot*, **booths** or shelters: brush-and-branch structures, temporary by definition. He builds himself a *bayit*, a house, the first time in the book anybody builds one for himself, and makes *sukkot* for the cattle — and names the place after the cattle shelters rather than the house. This is the word behind the Festival of Booths, the week Israel spends living in shelters to remember having no house.
- **v18 "came whole"** — *va-yavo Ya'aqov shalem*, and *shalem* is the adjective from *shalom*: whole, intact, unharmed, at peace, nothing missing. It can also be read as a place name. If it is the adjective, and that is the natural reading, it is the verdict on everything since he ran from Esau with nothing: twenty years with Laban, the flight, the pursuit, the night at the ford, the four hundred men — and he arrives **whole**, limping, but with nothing missing. The last line of the Jacob-and-Esau story is one word.
- **v18 "before the face of the city"** — *va-yichan et pnei ha-ir*. *Panim* once more, the word chapter 32 was built on, and now it is a city's face he camps in front of. The city is Shechem, and chapter 34 happens there.
- **v19 "a hundred qesitah"** — *qesitah* is a unit of money that appears three times in the entire Hebrew Bible and is never defined; nothing in the language fixes its value, and it is not used again after the oldest layers of the text. What the verse establishes is not the price but the fact: Jacob **buys** his ground, from named sellers, like Abraham buying the cave in chapter 23. The two purchases are the only land in Canaan the family owns.
- **v20 "El, God of Israel"** — *El Elohei Yisra'el*. He names the altar for the name he was given at the ford, and it is the first time *Israel* is used for anything beyond the man himself: not *my God* but *the God of Israel*, the form the whole nation will use. Twenty-four hours after being renamed in the dark by someone who would not give a name in return, he builds a stone thing and puts the new name on it.
