# Genesis 10

**1** These are the begettings of the sons of Noah — Shem, Ham, and Japheth — and sons were born to them after the flood.

## Japheth

**2** The sons of Japheth: Gomer and Magog and Madai and Javan and Tubal and Meshech and Tiras.

**3** The sons of Gomer: Ashkenaz and Riphath and Togarmah.

**4** The sons of Javan: Elishah and Tarshish, Kittim and Dodanim.

**5** From these the coastlands of the nations separated out in their lands, each by his tongue, by their families, in their nations.

## Ham

**6** The sons of Ham: Cush and Egypt and Put and Canaan.

**7** The sons of Cush: Seba and Havilah and Sabtah and Raamah and Sabteca. The sons of Raamah: Sheba and Dedan.

**8** Cush fathered Nimrod. He began to be a mighty one in the earth.

**9** He was a mighty hunter before the LORD. Therefore it is said, "Like Nimrod, a mighty hunter before the LORD."

**10** The beginning of his kingdom was Babel and Erech and Akkad and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.

**11** From that land went out Asshur, and he built Nineveh and Rehoboth-Ir and Calah,

**12** and Resen between Nineveh and Calah — that is the great city.

**13** Egypt fathered the Ludim and the Anamim and the Lehabim and the Naphtuhim,

**14** and the Pathrusim and the Casluhim — from whom the Philistines went out — and the Caphtorim.

**15** Canaan fathered Sidon his firstborn, and Heth,

**16** and the Jebusite and the Amorite and the Girgashite,

**17** and the Hivite and the Arkite and the Sinite,

**18** and the Arvadite and the Zemarite and the Hamathite. Afterward the families of the Canaanite were scattered.

**19** The border of the Canaanite was from Sidon, as you come toward Gerar, as far as Gaza; as you come toward Sodom and Gomorrah and Admah and Zeboiim, as far as Lasha.

**20** These are the sons of Ham, by their families, by their tongues, in their lands, in their nations.

## Shem

**21** To Shem, to him also, children were born — the father of all the sons of Eber, the brother of Japheth the elder.

**22** The sons of Shem: Elam and Asshur and Arpachshad and Lud and Aram.

**23** The sons of Aram: Uz and Hul and Gether and Mash.

**24** Arpachshad fathered Shelah, and Shelah fathered Eber.

**25** To Eber were born two sons. The name of the one was Peleg, for in his days the earth was divided; and the name of his brother was Joktan.

**26** Joktan fathered Almodad and Sheleph and Hazarmaveth and Jerah,

**27** and Hadoram and Uzal and Diklah,

**28** and Obal and Abimael and Sheba,

**29** and Ophir and Havilah and Jobab. All these were the sons of Joktan.

**30** Their dwelling was from Mesha, as you come toward Sephar, the mountain of the east.

**31** These are the sons of Shem, by their families, by their tongues, in their lands, by their nations.

**32** These are the families of the sons of Noah, by their begettings, in their nations. From these the nations separated out in the earth after the flood.

## Notes

- **vv5, 20, 31 "by their tongues"** — *li-lshonotam*, three times, once at the close of each branch. The nations here already have separate **languages**, and they already have separate lands. The next chapter opens with the whole earth speaking one lip and one set of words, and ends with languages being divided at Babel. The two chapters do not run in sequence. Chapter 10 is the outcome; chapter 11 goes back and tells how it happened. Hebrew narrative does this often — finish a line, then step back — and English readers who expect chapters to follow one another in time find a contradiction where the text intends an overlap.
- **v5 "separated out"** — *niphredu*, and the same verb closes the chapter in v32. The word for what happens to the nations is separation, the neutral term you would use of branches dividing. Nothing here is a punishment yet.
- **v8 "He began"** — *hechel*. This verb has been quietly marking the chapter turns: *then it was begun* to call on the name of the LORD (4:26), humankind *began* to be many (6:1), Noah *began* as a man of the ground (9:20), and now Nimrod *begins* to be a mighty one. Four beginnings, and the last two go badly.
- **v8, 9 "a mighty one… a mighty hunter"** — *gibbor*, the same word as *ha-gibborim* in 6:4, the mighty ones born before the flood who were "the men of the name." The flood was supposed to have ended them. Four verses after the last genealogy, the word is back.
- **v9 "before the LORD"** — *lifnei YHWH*, which ordinarily means *in the presence of* and carries no criticism at all. Every sinister reading of Nimrod — hunter of men, rebel, tyrant — comes from later tradition, not from these two verses. What the Hebrew says is that he was famous enough to become a proverb, and that his kingdom began at Babel.
- **v10 "Babel"** — named here, in the land of Shinar, before chapter 11 gets to it. The reader is told whose city it is before being told what happened there.
- **v11 "From that land went out Asshur"** — the Hebrew can equally be read *from that land he went out to Asshur*, with Nimrod as the subject continuing from v10. *Asshur* is both a personal name and the land of Assyria, and nothing in the grammar decides it. The choice changes who founded Nineveh.
- **v21 "the brother of Japheth the elder"** — *achi Yephet ha-gadol*. The adjective sits at the end and can attach to either brother: Shem, the elder brother of Japheth, or the brother of Japheth the elder. English translations split on it, usually silently. The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous about which of Noah's sons was the firstborn.
- **v25 "Peleg"** — the verse explains the name in the same breath: *for in his days the earth was divided*, *niphlegah*, from the root *palag*. Name and reason are the same word in Hebrew and unrelated sounds in English. Note also that *palag* is not the *parad* of vv5 and 32 — a different word for a different dividing, and the text does not say which one it has in mind.
- **The shape** — Japheth gets four verses, Ham gets fifteen, Shem gets eleven. The branch that matters least to the rest of the book is dealt with first and fastest; the line the book will follow is saved for last. That ordering — youngest or least first, chosen last — is how this book introduces nearly everyone.
