# Genesis 37

**1** Jacob settled in the land where his father had lived as a guest, in the land of Canaan.

**2** These are the generations of Jacob. Joseph, a son of seventeen years, was shepherding with his brothers among the flock — and he was a lad — with the sons of Bilhah and the sons of Zilpah, his father's wives. Joseph brought a bad report of them to their father.

**3** Israel loved Joseph more than all his sons, for he was a son of his old age to him, and he made him a long coat.

**4** His brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, and they hated him, and they could not speak to him in peace.

## Two Dreams

**5** Joseph dreamed a dream and told it to his brothers, and they hated him all the more.

**6** He said to them, "Listen, please, to this dream that I dreamed.

**7** And here — we were binding sheaves of grain in the middle of the field, and here, my sheaf rose and also stood upright, and here, your sheaves gathered around and bowed down to my sheaf."

**8** His brothers said to him, "Will you really reign over us? Will you really rule us?" They hated him all the more, over his dreams and over his words.

**9** He dreamed yet another dream and recounted it to his brothers, and said, "Here, I have dreamed a dream again, and here — the sun and the moon and eleven stars were bowing down to me."

**10** He recounted it to his father and to his brothers, and his father rebuked him and said to him, "What is this dream that you dreamed? Shall we really come — I and your mother and your brothers — to bow down to you, to the ground?"

**11** His brothers were jealous of him. But his father kept it in mind.

## To Dothan

**12** His brothers went to pasture their father's flock in Shechem.

**13** Israel said to Joseph, "Are your brothers not pasturing in Shechem? Come, and let me send you to them." Joseph said to him, "Here I am."

**14** Israel said to him, "Go, please, and see how your brothers are, and how the flock is, and bring me back word." He sent him from the valley of Hebron, and he came to Shechem.

**15** A man found him, and here — he was wandering in the field. The man asked him, saying, "What are you looking for?"

**16** He said, "I am looking for my brothers. Tell me, please, where they are pasturing."

**17** The man said, "They have moved on from here, for I heard them saying, 'Let us go to Dothan.'" And Joseph went after his brothers and found them at Dothan.

**18** They saw him from far off, and before he came near to them they schemed against him, to put him to death.

**19** They said, each man to his brother, "Here comes this master of dreams.

**20** And now, come, let us kill him and throw him into one of the pits, and we will say a wild animal ate him. We shall see what becomes of his dreams."

**21** Reuben heard, and he delivered him from their hand, and said, "Let us not take his life."

**22** Reuben said to them, "Do not shed blood. Throw him into this pit that is in the wilderness, and do not send a hand against him" — Reuben meaning to save him from their hands and bring him back to his father.

**23** And it came to pass, when Joseph came to his brothers, that they stripped Joseph of his coat, the long coat that was on him.

**24** They took him and threw him into the pit. The pit was empty; there was no water in it.

**25** They sat down to eat bread. They lifted their eyes and looked, and here — a caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead, and their camels carrying gum, balm, and ladanum, going to bring them down to Egypt.

**26** Judah said to his brothers, "What profit is it that we kill our brother and cover his blood?

**27** Come, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let our hand not be against him, for he is our brother, our flesh." His brothers listened.

**28** Midianite men, traders, passed by, and they drew Joseph up out of the pit and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver. They brought Joseph to Egypt.

## Identify, Please

**29** Reuben returned to the pit, and here — Joseph was not in the pit. Reuben tore his clothes.

**30** He returned to his brothers and said, "The boy is gone! I — where can I turn?"

**31** They took Joseph's coat and slaughtered a male goat and dipped the coat in the blood.

**32** They sent the long coat and brought it to their father, and said, "This we found. Identify, please — is it your son's coat or not?"

**33** He identified it and said, "My son's coat. A wild animal ate him. Torn, Joseph is torn."

**34** Jacob tore his clothes and put sackcloth on his loins, and mourned for his son many days.

**35** All his sons and all his daughters rose to comfort him, and he refused to be comforted, and said, "For I will go down to my son, mourning, to Sheol." His father wept for him.

**36** The Medanites sold Joseph into Egypt, to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, chief of the slaughterers.

## Notes

- **v2 "These are the generations of Jacob"** — *elleh toldot Ya'aqov*, and the next word is not a list. It is *Joseph*. This formula has organized the whole book — 2:4, 5:1, 6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 11:27, 25:12, 25:19, and twice in the chapter just finished, 36:1 and 36:9 — and every previous time it introduced a genealogy. This is the last one in Genesis, and what follows it is thirteen chapters of story. Jacob's *toldot* turn out to be a life, not a list.
- **v2 "a bad report of them"** — *dibbatam ra'ah*. *Dibbah* is not neutral information; it is the word for a whispering campaign, a defaming report. And the Hebrew is *their dibbah* — which can mean the report **about** them or the report **belonging to** them, so whether Joseph carried a true story or manufactured one is not settled by the grammar. What the verse does settle is that the first thing we learn about Joseph is that he informed on his brothers to their father.
- **v3 "a son of his old age"** — *ben zequnim*, and Benjamin is younger. The text gives this as Jacob's reason and lets it be wrong on its face: the actual son of his old age is the one whose birth killed Rachel. What is true is that Joseph is her first, and the verse before called Bilhah and Zilpah *his father's wives*, not servant girls, which is the only place in Genesis they are called that.
- **v3 "a long coat"** — *ketonet passim*, and the famous "coat of many colors" comes from the Greek and Latin translations, not from the Hebrew. *Pas* is the flat of the hand or the sole of the foot, so the likeliest sense is a tunic reaching to the palms and ankles — long-sleeved and full-length, a garment for someone who is not going to be doing work in it.

  There is one other *ketonet passim* in the entire Hebrew Bible, and it settles the register. 2 Samuel 13:18: *and on her was a ketonet passim, for so the virgin daughters of the king were clothed*. It is Tamar's garment, in the chapter where her brother rapes her — and she tears it. Both of the Bible's two *passim* coats are worn by a favored child, and both are destroyed by siblings.
- **vv4, 14 "peace"** — *shalom*, twice, and it is the cruellest pairing in the chapter. In v4 the brothers *could not speak to him **le-shalom***, could not say a peaceable word to him. In v14 Jacob sends him out with *go, please, see the **shalom** of your brothers*. The father sends the boy to enquire after the welfare of men who cannot say hello to him.
- **vv5, 8 "they added still more to hating him"** — *va-yosifu od sno oto*, and the verb is *yasaf*, **to add** — which is the verb Joseph's own name is made from. Rachel named him in 30:24 saying *may the LORD **add** me another son*. Twice in four verses, the text says his brothers *added* to their hatred, using his name as the verb. Hebrew does this without a wink; in English it cannot be heard at all.
- **v8 "Will you really reign... really rule"** — *ha-malokh timlokh... im mashol timshol*, both verbs doubled in the infinitive-absolute construction Hebrew uses for emphasis or incredulity. Two kingship words in one breath. The brothers have understood the dream exactly, and their objection is not that it is nonsense but that it is intolerable.
- **v10 "I and your mother"** — Rachel died in 35:19, two chapters ago. Jacob names her anyway in his rebuke, and the text does not comment, does not correct him, and never mentions her again in this chapter. The second dream has eleven stars, one for each brother; the moon has nobody left to be.
- **v11 "his father kept the matter"** — *shamar et ha-davar*, to guard, to keep, to hold on to. This is Jacob's third silence in four chapters: he *kept silent* when Dinah was raped (34:5), he *heard* about Reuben and Bilhah and said nothing (35:22), and now he rebukes his son out loud and then quietly files the dream away. The narrator tells us what he did with it and not what he thought.
- **v12 "to pasture their father's flock"** — the two letters of the word *et*, the object marker, carry a **dot over each of them** in the Hebrew manuscripts. It is the same kind of scribal mark that sits over *and he kissed him* in 33:4 — a signal made very early, copied ever since, and never explained in the text. This is the second one in five chapters.
- **vv15–17 the man in the field** — *va-yimtsa'ehu ish*, **a man found him**, wandering. He has no name, no reason to be there, no connection to anyone in the book, and he never appears again. He gets three verses — more than several of Jacob's sons get in their lifetimes — and all he does is overhear where the brothers went and pass it on. Joseph was at Shechem, where his brothers were supposed to be and were not, and he would have turned around. Instead a stranger sends him to Dothan. The text neither comments on this nor calls him an angel.
- **v19 "this master of dreams"** — *ba'al ha-chalomot ha-lazeh*. *Ba'al* is owner, husband, master of a thing; *ha-lazeh* is a contemptuous demonstrative, roughly *that one over there*. It is a sneer built out of two ordinary words, and it is one of the Bible's better insults: not *the dreamer* but **the dream-proprietor**.
- **v20 "we shall see what becomes of his dreams"** — the brothers say it as the last word of their plan, and the remaining thirteen chapters of Genesis are the answer. The sentence is not marked as irony by the narrator in any way. It is simply left there.
- **vv21–22 Reuben"** — *va-yatsilehu mi-yadam*, **he delivered him from their hand**, the verb *natsal* that has run through Jacob's story since 31:9. Reuben's plan is to have it both ways: no blood, a pit, and a quiet rescue later. It is the firstborn's instinct — he is the one with the most to answer for, and 35:22 already took his standing away. His concern surfaces plainly in v30: *the boy is gone. And I — where can I go?*
- **v24 "The pit was empty; there was no water in it"** — the narrator says it twice in six words. Hebrew doubles for emphasis, and what is being emphasized here is absence. The practical reading is that a dry cistern holds a boy alive; the sentence's own weight is on the nothing in it.

  And *bor*, pit, is the word Joseph himself uses in 40:15 from an Egyptian prison: *they put me in the **bor***. The story puts him in the same hole twice, and both times his brothers' doing is what put him there.
- **v25 "They sat down to eat bread"** — the next sentence after throwing him in. No comment is made. It is the single coldest line in the chapter, and it works because the narrator declines to do anything with it.
- **vv25, 28, 36 Ishmaelites, Midianites, Medanites** — three different names for the traders in twelve verses, and the grammar of v28 will not resolve them: *and Midianite men, traders, passed by, and **they** drew Joseph up out of the pit and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites* — with no way to tell from the Hebrew whether *they* is the brothers or the Midianites. Then v36 says the **Medanites** sold him in Egypt, one letter different from Midianites. It is a genuine tangle in the text, and it is not this rendering's job to smooth it out.

  What can be said, and it is checkable, is who all three are. Ishmael is Abraham's son by Hagar. And 25:2 lists Keturah's sons to Abraham: *Zimran, Jokshan, **Medan** and **Midian**, Ishbak and Shuah*. Every name attached to the caravan that carries Joseph to Egypt belongs to Abraham's own line. The family sells its son to its cousins.
- **v28 "twenty pieces of silver"** — Leviticus 27:5 sets the valuation of a male between five and twenty years old at **twenty shekels**. Joseph is seventeen (v2). The brothers get exactly the going rate, which is the detail that makes the transaction worse rather than better: it was not a panic sale.
- **vv31–33 the goat and the recognizing** — this is chapter 27 turned back on the man who did it. There, Rebekah dressed Jacob in *the skins of the kids of the goats* (27:16) and sent him to a father who could not see, and the text says *and he did **not recognize** him* — *ve-lo **hikkiro*** (27:23) — and gave him the blessing. Here his sons kill a male goat, dip the coat in its blood, and send it to him with two words: *hakker na*, **identify, please** (v32). And *va-yakkirah*, **and he identified it** (v33). Same verb, *nakhar*, both times; goat both times; a garment used to fake an identity both times. Jacob deceived his father with a goat and was not recognized. His sons deceive him with a goat and make him do the recognizing himself.

  And the two words *hakker na* are said to a man one more time in this book, in the very next chapter: Tamar sends them to Judah in 38:25, with his own staff and seal in her hand. Judah is the brother who proposed the sale in v27.
- **v33 "Torn, Joseph is torn"** — *tarof toraf Yosef*, the doubled verb again, and the root is *taraf*, the word for an animal killed by a predator. Its noun, *terefah*, is what Jacob threw at Laban in 31:39: *torn flesh I did not bring to you — I made it good myself*. For twenty years he absorbed every *terefah* in the herd rather than claim it. Now he believes his son is one, and there is nobody to make it good.
- **v35 "to Sheol"** — *she'olah*, and this is the first appearance of the word in Genesis, and so the first in the Bible. *She'ol* is where the dead are, and the Hebrew Bible says remarkably little about it: not a place of punishment, not yet a judgment, simply down, and everyone goes. Jacob's sentence is that he will go down to his son still mourning — he expects to arrive there grieving rather than to be relieved of it.

  And *all his sons and all his daughters* rose to comfort him. Daughters, plural. Only one is ever named.
- **v36 "chief of the slaughterers"** — *sar ha-tabbachim*, from *tabach*, to butcher. English Bibles print "captain of the guard," which is what the office is, but the Hebrew title is literally the head butcher, and it is the last phrase of the chapter. *Saris*, here rendered **officer**, is the ordinary word for a court official and in later Hebrew means eunuch; in this verse it is a rank, and the story will have something to say about Potiphar's household soon enough.
- **vv25–36 three names for one caravan** — the traders are called **Ishmaelites** when the brothers spot them (v25) and when Judah proposes the sale (v27); **Midianites** in the middle of v28; **Ishmaelites** again four words later in the same verse; and **Medanites** in v36. Three different peoples, in twelve verses, for what the story treats as one passing caravan. The verse that does the selling is also the one verse where nobody is named as the seller: *and they drew Joseph up out of the pit and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites* — the Hebrew never says whether *they* is the brothers or the Midianites, and both readings are grammatical. This is the hinge of the whole book and it is the one sentence that will not say who did it.
- **v34 "Jacob tore his clothes" — the two names, and what they track** — this chapter has been calling him **Israel**: *Israel loved Joseph more than all his sons* (v3), *Israel said to Joseph, are your brothers not pasturing* (v13). The coat comes back and he is **Jacob** again for the rest of the scene.
  From here to the end of Genesis the book alternates constantly — **forty** occurrences of Jacob against **twenty-eight** of Israel — and twice the switch happens across a single sentence break, both times on a man pulling himself upright. *And the spirit of **Jacob** their father came alive. And **Israel** said, "Enough! Joseph my son is still alive"* (45:27–28). *And they told **Jacob**… and **Israel** braced himself and sat up on the bed* (48:2). He goes down as one name and comes up as the other.
  The same split runs across whole chapters. Refusing to let Benjamin go — *my son shall not go down with you* — he is Jacob five times in chapter 42 and Israel not once. Giving in — *if it must be so, then do this* — he is Israel three times in chapter 43 and Jacob not once. The seventy-name list at 46:8–27 says Jacob ten times; the blessing of Ephraim and Manasseh at 48:8–21 says Israel eight times. Where he is counted, tallied or handled, he is Jacob; where he decides, rises and blesses, he is Israel.
  It is not a rule about God being present. He blesses Pharaoh as **Jacob**, twice in four verses (47:7, 10). And in the one place God addresses him directly, the verse holds both names pointing opposite ways: *God spoke to **Israel** in the visions of the night, and said, "**Jacob, Jacob**"* (46:2).
- **v7 "sheaves"** — *alummim*, from *alam*, to bind. At harvest the cut stalks of grain are gathered into armfuls, tied around the middle, and stood on end in the field to finish drying — a sheaf is one of those bundles, roughly the size and shape of a person. That is what makes the dream work: bundles of grain are already standing upright in a field by the nature of the thing, so what the dream reports is not them standing but **whose** stands and what the others do about it.
