# Genesis 2

**1** And the heavens and the earth were finished, and all their array.

**2** And on the seventh day God finished the work that He had done, and He ceased on the seventh day from all the work that He had done.

**3** And God blessed the seventh day and set it apart, because on it He ceased from all His work, which God had created to make.

## The Ground and the Man

**4** These are the begettings of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made earth and heaven.

**5** No shrub of the field was yet in the earth, and no plant of the field had yet sprung up, for the LORD God had not sent rain upon the earth, and there was no human to serve the ground.

**6** But a flow would go up from the earth and water the whole face of the ground.

**7** And the LORD God shaped the human of dust from the ground, and blew into his nostrils the breath of lives, and the human became a living soul.

**8** And the LORD God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and there He put the human whom He had shaped.

**9** And the LORD God made grow from the ground every tree desirable to look at and good for food, and the tree of life in the middle of the garden, and the tree of the knowing of good and evil.

**10** And a river goes out from Eden to water the garden, and from there it divides and becomes four heads.

**11** The name of the first is Pishon; it is the one winding through all the land of Havilah, where there is gold.

**12** And the gold of that land is good; bdellium is there, and the onyx stone.

**13** And the name of the second river is Gihon; it is the one winding through all the land of Cush.

**14** And the name of the third river is Tigris; it is the one going east of Asshur. And the fourth river is the Euphrates.

**15** And the LORD God took the human and set him down in the garden of Eden, to serve it and to keep it.

**16** And the LORD God laid a charge upon the human, saying, "From every tree of the garden you may surely eat.

**17** But from the tree of the knowing of good and evil you shall not eat, for on the day you eat from it, dying you shall die."

## The One Facing Him

**18** And the LORD God said, "It is not good for the human to be alone. I will make for him a help as his counterpart."

**19** And the LORD God shaped from the ground every living thing of the field and every bird of the heavens, and brought them to the human to see what he would call them; and whatever the human called each living soul, that was its name.

**20** And the human gave names to all the beasts, and to the birds of the heavens, and to every living thing of the field. But for the human he found no help as his counterpart.

**21** And the LORD God made a deep sleep fall upon the human, and he slept; and He took one of his sides and closed up the flesh in its place.

**22** And the LORD God built the side that He had taken from the human into a woman, and He brought her to the human.

**23** And the human said,  
"This one, this time, is bone of my bones  
and flesh of my flesh.  
This one shall be called Woman,  
for from Man was this one taken."

**24** Therefore a man leaves his father and his mother and clings to his woman, and they become one flesh.

**25** And the two of them were naked, the human and his woman, and they were not ashamed.

## Notes

- **v4 "begettings"** — Heb. *toledot*, the word that structures the whole book: *these are the begettings of Noah, of Terah, of Isaac*. It normally introduces a family line. Here it is applied to the sky and the ground, as though heaven and earth were parents with descendants. Notice also that the verse ends by reversing itself: it opens with *the heavens and the earth* and closes with *earth and heaven*. The order flips inside one verse, and the whole account that follows is told from the ground up.
- **v4 "the LORD God"** — the divine name YHWH appears for the first time here, paired with *Elohim*. Chapter 1 used *Elohim* alone, thirty-five times. The shift is abrupt and deliberate; whatever one concludes from it, an English reader should at least be able to see it happen.
- **v7 "shaped the human of dust from the ground"** — Heb. *va-yitser et-ha'adam 'aphar min-ha'adamah*. Two things English drops. First, *yatsar* is the potter's verb — the word used of a craftsman throwing clay, not of a decree. Second, *adam* (human) and *adamah* (ground) are the same word twice: he is an **earthling from the earth**, a groundling from the ground. Every English translation loses this, and it is the key to the whole chapter — the man is made of the stuff he is told to serve, and in 3:19 he goes back into it.
- **v7 "the breath of lives"** — *nishmat chayyim*, plural. Not a slip; Hebrew often speaks of life in the plural, the way we say "riches."
- **v7 "became a living soul"** — *nephesh chayyah*, the identical phrase used of the sea creatures, birds, and beasts in 1:20–30. The King James prints "living creature" there and "living soul" here. Same two words. The human's distinction in this verse is not the soul — the animals have that too — it is the breath, blown in personally.
- **v8 "a garden in Eden"** — *gan be-'eden*. *Eden* is not a place-name meaning "garden"; as a common noun it carries luxuriance, delight, abundance. And "paradise" is not in this text at all: that word entered through Greek *paradeisos*, borrowed from Persian, meaning a walled royal park.
- **v9 "the knowing of good and evil"** — *tov wa-ra*, a merism like "heaven and earth" in 1:1: naming two poles to mean the whole range between them. Closer to *knowing everything there is to know* than to a lesson in ethics.
- **v15 "to serve it and to keep it"** — *le-'ovdah u-le-shomrah*. *'Avad* is to serve, to labour for, and in religious contexts to worship; *shamar* is to guard, to keep watch over. "Till it and keep it" is not wrong but has gone soft. The man is placed in the garden as its servant and its watchman — and note that he was made to *serve the ground* back in v5, before there was a garden at all.

  Both suffixes on those verbs are feminine (*-ah*, "it"), but *gan*, garden, is a masculine noun. Strictly, they do not agree with it. Some readers take them as pointing back to *adamah*, the ground, which is feminine — serve **her** and keep **her**.
- **v17 "dying you shall die"** — *mot tamut*, an infinitive absolute doubling the verb for force. It is the formula of a legal death sentence, the phrasing a court uses: *he shall surely be put to death*. Not "you will become mortal." A verdict.
- **v18 "a help as his counterpart"** — *'ezer ke-negdo*, two words that have been mistranslated into an entire social order. *'Ezer* is help or rescue, and it is used of **God** more often than of anyone else — *the LORD is my helper* — so it carries no subordination whatsoever. *Ke-negdo* is "like, opposite him," "corresponding to him," one standing face to face with him. "Helpmeet" and "helpmate" are not translations; they are an English accident, from the King James phrase *an help meet for him*, where "meet" meant *suitable*.
- **v21 "one of his sides"** — *tsela*. The word means **side** — it is what the tabernacle has, what a hill has, what a building has. It means "rib" nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. Rendered "rib" here because the Greek and then the Latin chose narrower words, and English inherited them. The Septuagint itself shows the choice being made: everywhere *tsela* is an architectural side it uses *klitos*, a general flank — but at this verse it switches to *pleura*, the **body's** side, which is also the word for ribs. Latin then took the last step to *costa*, rib and nothing else. "Side" also makes better sense of v23: the point is that she is the matching half of a whole that was split.
- **v22 "built"** — *banah*, the verb for constructing a building, used of no other creature. The man is shaped like pottery; the woman is built like architecture.
- **v23 "Woman … Man"** — *ishshah* from *ish*. The pun survives into English better than most, which is why it is kept, but the Hebrew is tighter: one is the other with a syllable added.
- **v25 "naked"** — *'arummim*. Hold on to the sound of this word; 3:1 opens with *'arum*, and the joke is deliberate. There is a further detail only the Hebrew shows: 2:25 spells it *'arummim*, matching the serpent's word almost exactly, but when they see their nakedness in 3:7 it is spelled *'erummim* — the same word, written differently. The pun is set up with one spelling and paid off with another.
