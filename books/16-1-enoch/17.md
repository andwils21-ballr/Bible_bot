# 1 Enoch 17

**1** They took me away to a place where those who were there became like burning fire, and when they wished, they appeared like men.

**2** They brought me to a dark place, and to a mountain whose summit reached into heaven.

**3** I saw the places of the luminaries, and the storehouses of the stars and of the thunders, and into the airy depths, where the bow of fire was, and the arrows and their quivers, and all the lightnings.

**4** They took me as far as the waters of life, and as far as the fire of the west, which receives every setting of the sun.

**5** We came to a river of fire, in which the fire runs down like water and pours out into a great sea toward the west.

**6** I saw the great rivers, and I came as far as the great darkness, and I went where all flesh walks.

**7** I saw the winds of the winter darkness, and the pouring out of the abyss of all the waters.

**8** I saw the mouth of the earth, of all the rivers, and the mouth of the abyss.

## Notes

- **On these chapters** — the trial of the Watchers is over. From here to chapter 36 the book is a travelogue: Enoch is taken around the edges of the world and shown how it is put together. What follows is the oldest surviving map of the cosmos in Jewish literature, and it is given as eyewitness report.
- **v1 "became like burning fire, and when they wished, they appeared like men"** — the first thing he is shown is not a place but a capacity. These beings have no fixed form; the human shape is one they put on. It is the quiet answer to how two hundred of them married women in chapter 6.
- **v4 "the waters of life"** — the Greek has just that. The Ge'ez has *the water of life **that speaks***, a phrase with no parallel and no obvious sense. Something has happened to the line in one tradition or the other, and there is no third witness to break the tie.
- **v5 "a river of fire... pours out into a great sea toward the west"** — a river of fire running west into the sea of the dead is the geography of the Greek underworld, where the Pyriphlegethon is literally the *blazing-fire river*. Whether the book borrowed it or arrived at it independently, this is the furniture of the classical afterlife, described by a Hebrew patriarch.
- **v6 "I went where all flesh walks"** — the plainest phrase in the chapter and the darkest. Not a country and not a name: the road everyone is on. The book has been describing storehouses and mountains, and then in eight words says he visited death.
- **v7–8 "the mouth of the abyss"** — the chapter ends at plumbing. Every river has a mouth, and so does the abyss, and Enoch has been shown where the water comes from and where it goes. The interest is not moral here; it is mechanical. Somebody wanted to know how the world works.
