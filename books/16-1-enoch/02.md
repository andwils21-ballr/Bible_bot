# 1 Enoch 2

**1** Observe all the works in heaven, how they do not change their paths — and the lights in heaven, how each one rises and sets, each ordered in its appointed time, and they appear at their festivals, and they do not overstep their own order.

**2** Look at the earth, and think carefully about the works that are done on it, from first to last: how none of God's works changes, and all of them appear to you.

**3** Look at the signs of summer and of winter, how the whole earth is filled with water, and the clouds pour out rain over it.

---

## Notes

- **v1 "Observe"** — the first word of a sequence that runs from here to the end of chapter 5. Four times the reader is told to look at something in nature — the stars, the earth, the trees, the seasons — and each time the point is the same: they keep to their orders. The argument the book is building is that people do not.
- **v1 "each ordered in its appointed time, and they appear at their festivals"** — the Ge'ez stops halfway through this verse; the Greek carries it to the end. This is not decorative. The whole of 1 Enoch 72–82 is a solar calendar argued against the lunar one, and *they appear at their festivals* is the opening move: the feast days are set by the lights, and the lights do not drift.
- **v2 "Look at the earth"** — the Ge'ez has two words, **Look at**, and then nothing. The Greek continues: *and consider the works that are in it*. The **Aramaic** from Qumran has it too, and adds a word the others lack — *Co[ntemplate] the earth and o[bse]rve **its** works* — the earth's own works, not God's works on the earth.
- **v3 "the signs of summer and of winter"** — the word **signs** is in the Aramaic only. The Ge'ez and Greek both say simply *summer and winter*; the Aramaic says *the signs of* summer and *the signs of* winter. A sign is something meant to be read. The oldest witness makes the seasons a text, and the two later ones make them weather.
- **v3 "the clouds pour out rain"** — the Aramaic is the longest witness here, the only one of the three that names the clouds pouring. The Greek breaks off mid-verse and the Ge'ez gives a list — *water, and cloud and dew and rain* — without a verb.
