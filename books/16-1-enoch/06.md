# 1 Enoch 6

**1** And it came to pass, when the sons of men had multiplied in those days, that beautiful daughters were born to them, and lovely.

**2** And the angels, the sons of heaven, saw them and desired them, and said to one another, "Come, let us choose wives for ourselves from the sons of men, and let us father children for ourselves."

**3** And Semyaza, who was their leader, said to them, "I am afraid you will not want this deed done, and I alone will pay the penalty for this great sin."

**4** And they all answered him and said, "Let us all swear an oath, and bind ourselves to one another under a curse, that we will not turn back from this plan, but will carry this plan out in fact."

**5** Then they all swore together, and all bound themselves to one another under a curse upon it.

**6** And they were two hundred in all, and they came down onto Ardis, which is the summit of Mount Hermon. And they called it Mount Hermon, because they had sworn upon it and bound themselves under mutual curses.

**7** And these are the names of their chiefs: Semyaza, who was their leader, Urakibarameel, Akibeel, Tamiel, Ramuel, Danel, Ezeqeel, Saraquyal, Asael, Armaros, Batarael, Ananel, Zaqeel, Samsapeel, Satarel, Turel, Yomyael, Arazyal.

**8** These are the chiefs of the two hundred angels — the chiefs of the tens.

## Notes

- **v1 "when the sons of men had multiplied... beautiful daughters were born to them"** — this is **Genesis 6:1** almost word for word: *And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the ground, and daughters were born to them.* Genesis gives the episode four verses and moves on to the flood. 1 Enoch gives it ten chapters. Whichever direction the borrowing ran, the two books tell the same story and only one of them explains it.
- **v2 "the angels, the sons of heaven"** — Genesis 6:2 calls them *bene ha-Elohim*, **the sons of God**, and says nothing more about who they are. This book calls them angels in its second sentence on the subject and never treats the question as open.
- **v3 "I alone will pay the penalty"** — the leader's objection is not that the plan is wrong. It is that he will be left holding it. The oath in the next verse is his fix: make everyone equally guilty. The book's account of how a group does something none of them would do alone is four verses long and has not dated.
- **v6 "they called it Mount Hermon, because they had sworn upon it"** — the Ge'ez explains the name outright, and the explanation only works in a Semitic language. Hebrew *cherem* means something placed under a ban or a binding curse; that is the word for what the two hundred have just done to each other in vv4–5. So the mountain is named for the oath sworn on it. The Greek witness names Mount Hermoniem but does not give the etymology, and the **Aramaic** fragment breaks off just before the name. The complete Ethiopic text preserves a wordplay reaching back through a language it is not written in.
- **v6 "Ardis"** — the Ge'ez treats Ardis not as an alternative to Hermon but as the name of its summit. The **Greek** and the **Aramaic** instead date the descent — *in the days of Jared* — where the Ethiopic locates it.
- **v6 "in the days of Jared"** — in Genesis 5:18 Jared is Enoch's father. The name in Hebrew is *Yered*, from the verb *yarad*, **to come down**. The Watchers come down in the days of Descent, and the Aramaic has the same consonants for the name and for the act. It is one of the few wordplays in this book checkable in an original language rather than through a translation — and, like the Hermon one, it is a pun that only exists in the Semitic.
- **v7 "Urakibarameel"** — the list has eighteen names. The next verse calls these the chiefs of the tens, and two hundred split into tens needs twenty. This name looks like two run together, which would make nineteen. The count in the text we have does not reach the number the text itself gives.
- **v8 "the chiefs of the two hundred angels — the chiefs of the tens"** — the Ge'ez names the whole body of two hundred; the **Aramaic** gives two ranks, *These are the chiefs, and the chief[s] of the ten[s]*; the Greek gives only the tens. Three witnesses, three different answers to how the two hundred were organized.
