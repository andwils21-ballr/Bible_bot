# Wisdom of Solomon 13

**1** For all men who were ignorant of God were futile by nature;  
from the good things that are seen they could not know Him who is,  
nor by paying heed to His works did they recognize the craftsman,

**2** but they supposed that fire or wind or swift air,  
or the circle of the stars, or turbulent water,  
or the lights of heaven were the gods that rule the world.

**3** If it was through delight in their beauty that they took them to be gods,  
let them know how much better than these is their Master,  
for the author of beauty created them.

**4** If it was their power and working that amazed them,  
let them perceive from them  
how much more powerful is He who formed them.

**5** For from the greatness and beauty of created things  
their Creator is seen by analogy.

**6** Yet these men are little to be blamed,  
for perhaps they go astray  
while seeking God and wanting to find Him.

**7** For as they live among His works they search them out,  
and they trust what they see,  
because the things that are seen are beautiful.

**8** Yet again, not even they are to be excused;

**9** for if they had the power to know so much  
that they could explore the world,  
how did they not sooner find its Master?

**10** But miserable, with their hopes set on dead things,  
are those who called the works of men's hands gods:  
gold and silver shaped with skill,  
and likenesses of animals,  
or a useless stone, the work of an ancient hand.

**11** A woodcutter may saw down a tree that is easy to handle,  
skillfully strip off all its bark,  
and with pleasing workmanship  
make a useful vessel for the service of life,

**12** and burn the scraps of his work to cook his food,  
and eat his fill;

**13** but one of the leftover pieces, useful for nothing,  
a crooked stick full of knots,  
he takes and carves with care in his idle time,  
and shapes it with the skill of his leisure;  
he gives it the likeness of a man,

**14** or makes it like some worthless animal,  
smearing it with red ochre and painting its surface red,  
and covering over every flaw in it;

**15** then he makes a fitting shrine for it,  
and sets it in the wall, and fastens it with iron.

**16** So he takes care that it may not fall,  
knowing that it cannot help itself,  
for it is only an image, and needs help.

**17** When he prays about his possessions and his marriage and his children,  
he is not ashamed to speak to a lifeless thing;  
for health he calls on a thing that is weak;

**18** for life he asks a thing that is dead;  
for aid he begs a thing utterly without experience;  
for a journey, a thing that cannot even take a step;

**19** for gain and work and success with his hands  
he asks strength of a thing whose hands have no strength at all.

## Notes

- **v1 "Him who is"** — *ton onta*, the name God gives Himself in the Greek of Exodus 3:14, *I am He who is* (*ho ōn*). "The craftsman" — *technitēs*, the word used of wisdom as "fashioner" in 7:22.
- **vv1–9** — the first and gentlest of the book's charges: those who took fire, wind, stars or water for gods were looking for God in the right place and stopped short. Paul makes the same argument: *His invisible attributes… have been clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made* (Romans 1:20).
- **v5 "seen by analogy"** — *analogōs*, by proportion: the greater the work, the greater the maker.
- **v6 "while seeking God and wanting to find Him"** — compare Acts 17:27, *that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward Him and find Him*.
- **vv11–19 "A woodcutter… carves with care in his idle time"** — the scene of Isaiah 44:13–20, where one half of a tree cooks the meal and the other half is worshipped; here the god is the leftover, the knotted piece good for nothing else.
- **v15 "fastens it with iron"** — as in Isaiah 41:7 and Jeremiah 10:4, the idol nailed in place so that it will not totter.
- **vv17–19** — each prayer asks for the one thing the idol most lacks: health from the weak, life from the dead, a journey from what cannot walk, strength from hands that have none (*adranestaton… eudraneian*, "most strengthless… strength").
