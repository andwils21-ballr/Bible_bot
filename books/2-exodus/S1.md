    <h2>Notes on what's compressed, and what's left out</h2>
    <p><strong>Shapes.</strong> Square-cornered boxes are individual people the narrative follows. Pill shapes are peoples and nations &mdash; a name that has become a population. Dashed boxes bundle names that appear once in a genealogical list and are never seen again as characters; they are real, but the text gives them no story of their own.</p>
    <p><strong>The roots</strong> under the lowest name on each branch stand for the descendants the text never counts. Every line here runs on past the last box; the chart simply stops recording.</p>
    <p><strong>Why Jesus hangs under Judah.</strong> Both New Testament genealogies run him through Judah &mdash; Matthew from Abraham down (1:2&ndash;16), Luke from Jesus back up (3:23&ndash;38) &mdash; and both run through <em>Joseph</em>, not Mary. Matthew is careful about it. Thirty-nine times the chapter says <em>egennēsen</em>, <strong>fathered</strong>, in an unbroken chain; at 1:16 it stops. Joseph is called <em>the husband of Mary</em>, and the verb flips from active to passive: <em>of whom was born Jesus</em>. Luke puts the same caution in four words &mdash; <em>being son, <strong>as was supposed</strong>, of Joseph</em> (3:23). The line on this chart is real and legal, and the text will not call it biological.</p>
    <p><strong>Four women, and three of them come off this chart.</strong> Matthew names women in the descent, which genealogies of the period did not usually do: Tamar (1:3), Rahab of Jericho (1:5), <strong>Ruth the Moabite</strong> (1:5, and Ruth 1:4), and at 1:6 the wife of Uriah &mdash; <strong>Uriah the Hittite</strong> (2 Samuel 11:3) &mdash; who is the only one of the four Matthew leaves unnamed. Moab is on this tree under Lot. The Hittites are on it under Heth, on Ham's side. Rahab's Jericho is Canaan. The peoples this chart spends its width separating out turn up again inside the last line on it.</p>
    <p><strong>Moses and Aaron are brothers.</strong> Both are sons of Amram and Jochebed, grandsons of Kohath, great-grandsons of Levi (Exodus 6:16&ndash;20), and Aaron is the elder by three years (Exodus 7:7). Miriam is their sister: the Hebrew of Exodus 6:20 names only the two brothers, but Numbers 26:59 names all three, and the Greek of Exodus 6:20 adds her there too.</p>

    <p><strong>Mary's own connection, as far as the text goes.</strong> Luke says Elizabeth is Mary's <em>syngenis</em>, her relative (1:36), and that Elizabeth is <em>of the daughters of Aaron</em> (1:5) &mdash; which is Levi. Luke does not say how close the relation is, and no genealogy of Mary is given anywhere. That is the whole of it; anything more precise is supplied from outside the text.</p>
    <p><strong>Japheth's line is thinner than the others because Genesis gives less.</strong> Seven sons (10:2), then three sons of Gomer (10:3) and four of Javan (10:4) &mdash; and there it stops. Magog, Tubal, Meshech and Tiras get no descendants at all. The summary calls them <em>the coastlands of the nations</em> (10:5), a maritime rather than a territorial description. Two of the names are identified in the text later: Daniel calls the Greek king <em>the king of Javan</em> (8:21), and pairs <em>Madai</em> with Persia (5:28, 9:1).</p>
    <p><strong>Perizzite isn't on this tree at all.</strong> Unlike Hittite, Amorite, Hivite, and Jebusite, it never appears in Genesis 10's genealogy of Canaan. Its name most likely describes a way of life &mdash; people of the open, unwalled country &mdash; rather than a bloodline, so it has no ancestor to hang from here.</p>
    <p>This tree stops at what's directly relevant to Israel's own story. It leaves out the deeper reaches of Japheth's line, most of Genesis 36's long list of Edomite chiefs, and every genealogy past Jacob's twelve sons.</p>
