# Exodus 21

**1** And these are the rulings that you shall set before them.

## Slaves

**2** When you buy a Hebrew slave, he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free, for nothing.

**3** If he came in by himself, he shall go out by himself. If he was the husband of a wife, then his wife shall go out with him.

**4** If his master gives him a wife and she bears him sons or daughters, the wife and her children shall belong to her master, and he shall go out by himself.

**5** And if the slave says plainly, "I love my master, my wife, and my sons — I will not go out free,"

**6** then his master shall bring him to God, and shall bring him to the door or to the doorpost, and his master shall pierce his ear with an awl, and he shall serve him for life.

**7** And when a man sells his daughter as a slave woman, she shall not go out as the male slaves go out.

**8** If she is displeasing in the eyes of her master, who designated her for himself, he shall let her be redeemed. He has no right to sell her to a foreign people, since he has broken faith with her.

**9** And if he designates her for his son, he shall do for her according to the ruling for daughters.

**10** If he takes another for himself, he shall not withhold from her her food, her clothing, or her due.

**11** And if he does not do these three things for her, she shall go out for nothing, without payment.

## Capital Cases

**12** Whoever strikes a man so that he dies shall be put to death.

**13** But if he did not lie in wait, and God let it meet his hand, then I will appoint for you a place where he may flee.

**14** And when a man acts with intent against his neighbor, to kill him by cunning, you shall take him from My altar to die.

**15** And whoever strikes his father or his mother shall be put to death.

**16** And whoever steals a man and sells him, or he is found in his hand, shall be put to death.

**17** And whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death.

## Injury

**18** And when men quarrel and one strikes his neighbor with a stone or with a fist, and he does not die but takes to his bed —

**19** if he gets up and walks about outside on his staff, the one who struck him shall be clear. Only he shall pay for his sitting still, and shall pay for his healing.

**20** And when a man strikes his male slave or his female slave with a rod, and the slave dies under his hand, it shall be avenged.

**21** But if the slave stands up after a day or two, it shall not be avenged, for he is his money.

**22** And when two men fight and strike a pregnant woman, and her child comes out not yet formed, he shall be fined. As the woman's husband imposes on him, so he shall pay, by assessment.

**23** But if it was formed, he shall give life for life,

**24** eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot,

**25** burn for burn, wound for wound, bruise for bruise.

**26** And when a man strikes the eye of his male slave or the eye of his female slave and ruins it, he shall let the slave go free in place of the eye.

**27** And if he knocks out the tooth of his male slave or the tooth of his female slave, he shall let the slave go free in place of the tooth.

## The Goring Ox and the Open Pit

**28** And when an ox gores a man or a woman so that they die, the ox shall be stoned and its flesh shall not be eaten, and the owner of the ox is clear.

**29** But if the ox was a gorer from yesterday and the day before, and its owner was warned and did not keep it in, and it kills a man or a woman, the ox shall be stoned and its owner also shall be put to death.

**30** If a ransom is imposed on him, he shall give the redemption of his life according to all that is imposed on him.

**31** Whether it gores a son or gores a daughter, this same ruling shall be done for him.

**32** If the ox gores a male slave or a female slave, he shall give thirty shekels of silver to their master, and the ox shall be stoned.

**33** And when a man opens a pit, or when a man digs a pit and does not cover it, and an ox or a donkey falls into it,

**34** the owner of the pit shall make it good. He shall return silver to its owner, and the dead animal shall be his.

**35** And when one man's ox strikes his neighbor's ox and it dies, they shall sell the live ox and divide the silver, and they shall also divide the dead one.

**36** Or if it was known that the ox was a gorer from yesterday and the day before, and its owner did not keep it in, he shall make good, ox for ox, and the dead one shall be his.

## Notes

- **v1 "the rulings"** — *mishpatim*, from *shafat*, to judge. Not commandments and not statutes: **decisions**, the kind a court hands down. And the whole chapter is built on one sentence shape — *when* such a thing happens, *then* this is done — which is how a case is written up rather than how a rule is proclaimed. Chapter 20 said *you shall not*. Chapter 21 says *when a man's ox gores*. The two are different kinds of writing sitting back to back.
- **v3 "by himself"** — *be-gappo*, a word that occurs in the Hebrew Bible only here and in v4, twice in the same law. Something like *with his body only* — he arrived with nothing and leaves with nothing. Because nothing else in the language uses it, its exact force cannot be recovered; what the law does with it is clear enough, and brutal: v4 says a wife given to him by the master stays behind, and so do his children.
- **v6 "bring him to God… pierce his ear"** — *ha-elohim*, which can be God or the divine court, and the Greek settled it one way: *to the judgment-seat of God*. Then the man is put against the **door**, and the awl goes through his ear into the doorpost he will keep going through. And the term is *le-olam*, the word for the farthest horizon anyone can see — the same word in *El Olam*, God of Everlasting, at Genesis 21:33. Here it means: until you die.
- **v10 "her food, her clothing, or her due"** — the third item is *onatah*, and nobody can say for certain what it is. Proposals run from her conjugal right, to oil, to lodging, to her time — each argued from a different root, none decisive. What the law is doing is not in doubt: a man who takes a second wife owes the first three specific things, and v11 says failing any one of them buys her freedom outright. The obscure word is one of the three.
- **vv13–14 "God let it meet his hand… take him from My altar"** — the line between murder and accident is drawn twice and from both sides. On one side, an unintended killing is described as something **God** caused to meet the man's hand, and the Greek says the same: *God delivered it into his hands*. On the other, a man who killed *be-ormah*, by cunning, gets no refuge: the altar is named as the place he will be dragged away from. Holy ground shelters the one and not the other, and the difference is entirely what was in his head.
- **v16 "whoever steals a man"** — kidnapping carries the same sentence as murder, and the law is stated with no qualification. Set it against the end of this chapter: stealing an **ox** costs five oxen. Stealing a person costs your life.
- **vv20–21 "for he is his money"** — *ki khaspo hu*. The law limits what an owner may do — kill a slave with a rod and *it shall be avenged* — and then says that if the slave survives a day or two there is no case, and gives the reason in three words. Both halves are in the same paragraph. The verse restrains the owner and prices the slave in the same breath, and no English rendering can make that read comfortably.
- **vv22–23 the two witnesses ask different questions** — this rendering follows the **Greek**, and the two oldest texts of Exodus do not set the same test.

  The Greek turns on *exeikonismenon*, from *eikōn*, **image** or shape: the child comes out *mē exeikonismenon*, **not yet formed**, and the penalty is money; *but if it was formed*, the penalty is *psychēn anti psychēs*, **life for life**. That is a threshold, and it is a threshold a court could actually work: nobody in this world counts weeks, so the standard is read off the body — you look at what came out and see whether it had taken human shape. Neither verse contains a word for dying. It says only that the child **came out**, and in a world without any means of keeping a premature infant alive, a pregnancy that comes out early does not survive. What the Greek grades, then, is how far along the lost child had come.

  The **Hebrew** does not grade it. Its condition is *ason*, **harm** — no *ason*, a fine; *ason*, life for life — and it never says whose harm. If the children coming out is itself the loss, then the no-*ason* case **is** the dead child and a fine covers it, which leaves *ason* to mean harm to the woman. Read that way the Hebrew sets no threshold at all: the pregnancy is compensable at any stage, and only the woman is life for life. *Ason* is rare enough that nothing in the verse settles it; what fixes its weight is elsewhere in this canon, where it is the word Jacob keeps using about Benjamin — *harm might come to him* (Genesis 42:4, 42:38, 44:29).

  One asks how developed the child was. The other asks who got hurt. Neither is a version of the other, and both sit inside a chapter about what one man owes another when a fight goes wrong — the fine is set by the woman's husband, and paid to him.
- **vv24–27 "eye for eye"** — seven pairs run on the preposition *tachat*, **in place of**, in two verses, and the famous phrase is not a general principle here. It sits inside a single case about a fight and a miscarriage, and it caps what may be exacted rather than licensing it. Then the next two verses reuse the identical formula to a different end: an owner who destroys a slave's eye lets the slave go free **in place of the eye**. The compensation clause has become an emancipation clause with the same three words.
- **v32 "thirty shekels of silver"** — the fixed sum an ox's owner pays when the ox kills someone else's slave. It is the only place the law puts an exact price on a human being. The Greek writes it *thirty pieces of silver* — τριάκοντα, ἀργυρίου — and that is the figure the chief priests weigh out to Judas in Matthew 26:15: *thirty pieces of silver*, τριάκοντα ἀργύρια. The amount is not invented for the Gospel; it is the compensation this chapter sets for a dead slave.
