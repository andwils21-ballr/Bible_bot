# Sirach 30

**1** *Concerning children.*  
Whoever loves his son will whip him often,  
so that he may rejoice in him at the last.

**2** Whoever disciplines his son will profit from him,  
and will boast of him among his acquaintances.

**3** Whoever teaches his son will make his enemy jealous,  
and will rejoice in him before his friends.

**4** When his father dies, it is as though he had not died,  
for he has left behind him one like himself.

**5** In his life he saw him and rejoiced,  
and at his death he did not grieve;

**6** he has left behind an avenger against his enemies,  
and one to repay kindness to his friends.

**7** For his sons' lives he will bind up his wounds,  
and at every cry his heart will be shaken.

**8** An unbroken horse turns out stubborn,  
and a son left to himself turns out headstrong.

**9** Pamper a child, and he will terrify you;  
play with him, and he will grieve you.

**10** Do not laugh with him, or you will grieve with him,  
and in the end you will grind your teeth.

**11** Do not give him free rein in his youth;

**12** bruise his ribs while he is a child,  
or he will grow stubborn and disobey you.

**13** Discipline your son and take pains with him,  
so that he does not stumble, to your disgrace.

**14** Better a poor man who is healthy and strong in body  
than a rich man plagued in his body.

**15** Health and soundness are better than any gold,  
and a strong body than boundless wealth.

**16** *Concerning food.*  
There is no wealth better than health of body,  
and no gladness above joy of heart.

**17** Death is better than a bitter life,  
or a lasting illness.

**18** Good things poured out on a closed mouth  
are like offerings of food set by a grave.

**19** What good is an offering to an idol?  
It can neither eat nor smell.  
So is the man who is hounded by the Lord:

**20** he sees with his eyes and groans,  
like a eunuch who embraces a virgin and groans.

**21** Do not give yourself over to sorrow,  
and do not distress yourself with your own thoughts.

**22** Gladness of heart is the life of a man,  
and a man's joy is length of days.

**23** Love your soul and comfort your heart,  
and drive sorrow far from you;  
for sorrow has killed many,  
and there is no profit in it.

**24** Envy and anger shorten one's days,  
and anxiety brings old age before its time.

**25** A cheerful and good heart  
attends to the food it eats.

## Notes

- **v1 "*Concerning children*"** — a heading in the Greek text, as at v16, *Concerning food*. "Will whip him often" — *endelechēsei mastigas autō*, "will keep up lashes on him": Proverbs 13:24, *Whoever spares his rod hates his son*.
- **vv4–6 "it is as though he had not died, for he has left behind him one like himself"** — the son who carries on his father: he avenges his father's enemies and repays his father's friends.
- **v7 "For his sons' lives he will bind up his wounds"** — *peri psychōn huiōn katadesmeusei traumata autou*, as Swete prints it: the father's anxiety for his sons, set between the son who carries on his father (vv4–6) and the son left to himself (vv8–13).
- **v18 "offerings of food set by a grave"** — food laid at a tomb for the dead, who cannot eat it. Tobit 4:17 commands the custom as charity, *Pour out your bread on the grave of the righteous*; here it is the picture of good things wasted on a man who can no longer taste them.
- **v19 "It can neither eat nor smell"** — the idols of Deuteronomy 4:28, *which neither see nor hear nor eat nor smell*, and Psalm 115:6, *noses, but do not smell*. "Hounded by the Lord" — *ekdiōkomenos*, "driven out, pursued": the sick man who cannot eat the good things set before him.
- **v20 "like a eunuch who embraces a virgin"** — the image of 20:4, where it stands for justice done by force; here it is the sick man who holds good things and cannot enjoy them.
- **v23 "Love your soul"** — *agapa tēn psychēn sou*: care for your own life, the opposite of the sorrow that *has killed many*.
- **v25** — Swete prints this line at the end of v24. From here to chapter 36 the Greek text Swete prints has two blocks of chapters in exchanged order, and Swete numbers them as they stand: his 30:25–40 is English 33:16–31, his 31–33 are English 34:1–36:11, his 34–35 are English 31–32, and his 36 is English 33:1–16 with 36:11–26. The English numbering is followed.
