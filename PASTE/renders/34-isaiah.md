## Isaiah 1 — rendered 2026-10-09, 12:51 PM CDT

**Source:** Hebrew (WLC). English and Hebrew verse numbers agree. A new writer: the style is taken from the Hebrew, and the rules from the fixed models (Genesis 25, Exodus 33, Leviticus 17).

**Four section headings** mark the chapter's movements: The Rebel Sons, Your Sacrifices, Come, Let Us Settle This, The Faithful City.

Echoes in the notes were checked three ways:
- **Against this project's English where quoted:** Exodus 34:24; Deuteronomy 29:23, 32:1.
- **Against the Hebrew in `sources/`:** *mahpekhah* in Deuteronomy 29:22 (English 29:23), Isaiah 13:19, Jeremiah 49:18, 50:40 and Amos 4:11. The count of *qedosh Yisrael*: Isaiah 24; elsewhere 2 Kings 19:22, Jeremiah 50:29, 51:5, and Psalms 71:22, 78:41, 89:19 (English 89:18). Isaiah 57:5 and 65:3.
- **Against the Greek in `sources/`:** SBLGNT Romans 9:29.

**The checker** found nothing.

**What this chapter is:** the LORD's lawsuit against Judah. Heaven and earth are called as witnesses; the sons have rebelled; *the ox knows its owner*. The land is beaten from head to foot, and Zion is left like a shelter in a vineyard. Sacrifices are rejected from *hands full of blood*. *Come now, let us settle this.* The faithful city has become a prostitute, and will be called *the Faithful City* again.

### Choices

**Isaiah 1:18: promise or question?**

Reads now: "Come now, let us settle this," says the LORD: / "Though your sins are like scarlet, / they shall be white as snow; / though they are red like crimson, / they shall become like wool."

The Hebrew is *im yihyu chata'eikhem kashshanim kashsheleg yalbinu*: "if your sins are like scarlet, as snow they will be white."
1. **A promise** (as now): God offers to wash the sins white. This is the usual reading.
2. **A judge's challenge**: "If your sins are scarlet, shall they pass for white as snow?" The *if* is then a rebuke, and vv19–20 give the real terms: obey or be eaten by the sword. The chapter's frame is a lawsuit (*let us settle this*), which supports reading the line as part of the argument.

The grammar allows both. **Recommendation:** keep the promise, with the note giving the other reading. The line is too well known as a promise to change without strong reason, and the context does not force the question.
